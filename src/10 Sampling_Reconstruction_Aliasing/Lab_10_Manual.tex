\section{Real-World Context: Sampling Across Signal Acquisition Systems}
%--------------------------------------------------------------

\noindent
You are a Junior Signal Acquisition Engineer at a company designing data acquisition and test equipment. Your expertise in the Nyquist-Shannon sampling theorem is critical to three very different product lines, each handling signals at vastly different frequencies and with different consequences for aliasing errors.

\subsection{Program 1: High-Speed Digital Storage Oscilloscopes (Test \& Measurement)}

Your company manufactures digital oscilloscopes used by hardware engineers to debug circuits, validate prototypes, and characterize signal integrity in digital systems operating at gigahertz speeds.

\textbf{The challenge:} An oscilloscope must capture fast transients (microsecond-scale details) while also showing the overall signal envelope (millisecond or longer). The sampling rate determines what you can observe. Too slow, and fast edges appear blurry and aliased. Too fast, and you waste memory and storage bandwidth.

\textbf{The engineering problem:} A digital circuit operates at 1 GHz. To observe timing relationships and metastability events, you need to sample at $\geq 2 \times 1$ GHz = 2 GHz (Nyquist minimum). But actually, professional oscilloscopes use $\geq 5--10 \times$ the signal bandwidth to get clean eye diagrams and characterize jitter. Your job: design the sample-and-hold amplifier that feeds the analog-to-digital converter. Calculate Nyquist limits, predict alias frequencies if sampling is inadequate, and measure the analog reconstruction filter's cutoff frequency to prevent aliasing from entering the acquisition chain.

\textbf{Regulatory requirement:} Prove that your oscilloscope's sample-and-hold circuit introduces $< 5$ ns of delay variation (aperture jitter) across the temperature range, and that the reconstruction filter has $\geq 80$ dB attenuation at the Nyquist frequency to suppress aliases that would corrupt measurements.

\subsection{Program 2: High-Fidelity Audio Recording Systems (Music \& Entertainment)}

Your company manufactures digital audio interfaces for professional recording studios, concert venues, and broadcast stations. Recording engineers demand transparent, artifact-free audio capture.

\textbf{The challenge:} Human hearing spans approximately 20 Hz to 20 kHz. Professional audio standards use 44.1 kHz (CD), 48 kHz (video/film), or 96 kHz (mastering) sampling rates. The standard 44.1 kHz was chosen specifically to exceed the Nyquist rate ($2 \times 20$ kHz = 40 kHz). But at exactly 40 kHz Nyquist, any signal energy at 20 kHz aliases back to 0 Hz (DC). Real signals extend beyond 20 kHz (ultrasonic transients from instruments). A sharp anti-aliasing filter is critical.

\textbf{The engineering problem:} Design an anti-aliasing filter that passes 0--20 kHz transparently (with no phase distortion or group delay variation) while attenuating everything above 22 kHz by $> 90$ dB. This requires a high-order filter (4th--8th order butterworth or Chebyshev). Your lab models this filtering in Simulink and validates it empirically by sampling signals at various rates and listening for artifacts.

\textbf{Quality requirement:} Measure the alias rejection ratio: if you intentionally apply a 25 kHz tone (which should alias to 19 kHz under 44.1 kHz sampling), quantify how much energy appears at the alias frequency in the reconstructed signal. Specification: $< -80$ dB alias suppression (inaudible to trained ears).

\subsection{Program 3: Medical Sensor Data Acquisition (Biomedical Devices)}

Your company manufactures wearable and implantable biomedical sensors: ECG monitors, glucose meters, motion sensors, and respiratory monitors. Each sensor samples at a rate determined by the biological signal's bandwidth, power budget, and regulatory requirements.

\textbf{The challenge:} Heart rate (ECG) spans 0.5--100 Hz. Respiratory rate is 0.1--0.5 Hz. Motion (accelerometer) spans 0 Hz (static tilt) to $\sim$ 10 kHz (vibration). A glucose meter samples blood glucose once every 5 minutes. Each signal requires a different sampling strategy. Undersample and you miss events; oversample and you waste power (every sample drains the battery).

\textbf{The engineering problem:} Design a multi-rate sampling strategy for a wearable health monitor: ECG at 250 Hz (Nyquist for 100 Hz content, plus margin), motion at 100 Hz (sufficient for fall detection), glucose at 0.1 Hz (once per 10 minutes). Each signal must be anti-aliased before downsampling. Your lab implements a sample-and-hold circuit and demonstrates alias rejection. You'll measure how much motion artifact (noise) aliases into the ECG band when sampling too slowly.

\textbf{Clinical requirement:} Prove that your anti-aliasing filter preserves clinically relevant ECG features (ST-segment details, T-wave morphology) that occur at 10--50 Hz, while rejecting motion noise (artifacts above 200 Hz that would alias into the ECG band under slow sampling).

\subsection{The Unifying Principle: Nyquist, Reconstruction, and Aliasing Prevention}

All three programs depend on mastering the same fundamental concepts:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{The Sampling Theorem:} If a signal is band-limited to frequency $f_{\text{max}}$, you must sample at $f_s \geq 2 f_{\text{max}}$ (Nyquist rate) to preserve all information. Undersample, and frequency components above $f_s / 2$ alias down into the passband, corrupting the signal irreversibly.

    \item \textbf{Aliasing Prediction:} Hand-calculate where alias frequencies will appear. A signal at $f_0$ sampled at $f_s$ produces aliases at $|k f_s \pm f_0|$ for integer $k$.

    \item \textbf{Anti-Aliasing Filter Design:} Place an analog low-pass filter (cutoff at $f_s / 2$ or slightly lower) before the sampler to remove high-frequency content that would alias.

    \item \textbf{Reconstruction from Samples:} After digitization and processing, a digital-to-analog converter with a reconstruction filter returns the signal to continuous-time, approximating the original analog waveform.

    \item \textbf{Hardware Validation:} Build the sample-and-hold circuit, measure its performance, compare empirical results to Nyquist predictions, and document any deviations (due to real component tolerances, settling time, and noise).
\end{enumerate}

In this lab, you will build a sample-and-hold circuit controlled by an Arduino timer, sample analog signals at various rates, reconstruct with an RC low-pass filter, and prove that your measurements match the Nyquist-Shannon theorem.

\section{Core Concepts: Sampling, Nyquist, and Reconstruction}
%--------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{The Nyquist-Shannon Sampling Theorem}

A continuous-time signal $x(t)$ that contains no frequency components higher than $f_{\text{max}}$ (i.e., is \textit{band-limited}) can be completely reconstructed from a set of samples taken at equal intervals, provided the sampling rate $f_s$ satisfies:
\begin{equation}
f_s \geq 2 f_{\text{max}}.
\end{equation}

The minimum rate $f_s = 2 f_{\text{max}}$ is called the \textbf{Nyquist rate}. The quantity $f_{\text{Nyquist}} = f_s / 2$ is the \textbf{Nyquist frequency}—the highest frequency that can be represented without ambiguity at a given sampling rate.

\textbf{Consequence of undersampling:} If $f_s < 2 f_{\text{max}}$, frequency components above $f_s / 2$ are \textit{aliased}—they appear as lower-frequency components in the sampled signal, and the original signal cannot be reconstructed.

\subsection{Alias Frequencies}

When a continuous-time signal $x(t)$ containing a sinusoid at frequency $f_0$ is sampled at rate $f_s$, the discrete-time samples contain not only the original frequency but also \textit{aliases} at frequencies:
\begin{equation}
f_{\text{alias}} = |k f_s \pm f_0|, \quad k = 0, 1, 2, \ldots
\end{equation}

\textbf{Example:} A 25 kHz sinusoid sampled at 44.1 kHz produces aliases at:
\begin{align}
|1 \times 44.1 - 25| &= 19.1 \text{ kHz},\\
|2 \times 44.1 - 25| &= 63.2 \text{ kHz},\\
|0 \times 44.1 + 25| &= 25 \text{ kHz}, \quad \text{(original, above Nyquist)}.
\end{align}

The 19.1 kHz alias is in the audible range and will be heard if not filtered out.

\subsection{Reconstruction from Samples}

An ideal reconstructor uses the \textit{sinc interpolation} formula:
\begin{equation}
x(t) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} x[n] \, \text{sinc}\left( \pi f_s (t - nT_s) \right),
\end{equation}
where $x[n]$ are the sample values and $T_s = 1/f_s$ is the sample period.

In practice, a simple low-pass RC filter approximates this sinc interpolation, smoothing between sample values.

\subsection*{Arduino Timer Configuration}

The Arduino's Timer1 (16-bit) will be configured to generate periodic interrupt signals that control the CD4066 sampling switch. The sampling frequency is set by the Timer1 overflow rate:
\begin{equation}
f_s = \frac{f_{\text{clock}}}{N \times (\text{TOP} + 1)},
\end{equation}
where $f_{\text{clock}} = 16$ MHz, $N$ is the prescaler, and TOP is the timer's maximum count value.

\section{Safety and Wiring Notes}
%--------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{High-frequency signals:} Function generators operating above 100 kHz can couple into nearby circuits and create ground loops. Keep signal wires short and twisted.
    \item \textbf{Breadboard contact resistance:} At high frequencies (>10 kHz), contact resistance in breadboards can affect filter response. Use quality jumper wires and ensure solid connections.
    \item \textbf{Capacitor leakage current:} The sample-and-hold capacitor must have low leakage (use film or quality ceramic; avoid cheap electrolytics). Leakage causes the held voltage to decay between samples.
    \item \textbf{Op-amp settling:} Op-amp buffer following the sample-and-hold must settle quickly ($< 1$ µs for frequencies above 100 kHz). Use high-slew-rate op-amps (TL072, LM358, or faster).
    \item \textbf{Ground connections:} All grounds (Arduino, function generator, oscilloscope, breadboard) must be connected to a common point to avoid ground loops and noise coupling.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{CD4066 Analog Switch Basics}

The CD4066 is a CMOS quad bilateral switch—when a digital control signal is HIGH, it closes the switch (low resistance path); when LOW, it opens (high impedance). This allows the Arduino to connect/disconnect a signal path at precise timing intervals.

\textbf{For sampling:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Input: analog signal from function generator.
    \item Control: digital signal from Arduino Timer1 interrupt (HIGH for \textasciitilde 1 µs to sample, LOW to hold).
    \item Output (through capacitor): holds the sampled voltage.
    \item Buffer: op-amp follows to provide low-impedance output and prevent capacitor discharge.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Wiring Configuration}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Function generator} output → resistor (10 k$\Omega$) → CD4066 input pin (pin 1 for switch A).
    \item \textbf{CD4066 output} (pin 2) → capacitor (100 nF) to ground. This capacitor holds the sample.
    \item \textbf{Capacitor node} → non-inverting input of op-amp buffer (TL072 or LM358).
    \item \textbf{Op-amp} (configured as unity-gain buffer): output is the sampled signal, low impedance.
    \item \textbf{CD4066 control pin} (pin 13) ← Arduino Timer1 output (pin 11 on Uno, PWM or OC1A).
    \item \textbf{CD4066 power}: pins 14 (V$_{DD}$, +5V), pins 7 \& 8 (V$_{SS}$, GND).
    \item \textbf{Oscilloscope}: Channel 1 = function generator input. Channel 2 = op-amp output (sampled signal).
\end{enumerate}

\section*{Appendix A: Nyquist-Shannon Theorem (Reference)}

%--------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection*{A.1 Mathematical Statement}

Let $x(t)$ be a signal whose frequency content is limited to frequencies below $f_{\text{max}}$ (i.e., $X(f) = 0$ for $|f| > f_{\text{max}}$, where $X(f)$ is the Fourier transform of $x(t)$).

If samples are taken at a rate $f_s > 2 f_{\text{max}}$, then $x(t)$ can be uniquely recovered from the sample sequence $\{x(nT_s)\}_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}$, where $T_s = 1/f_s$.

\subsection*{A.2 Aliasing Under Undersampling}

If $f_s < 2 f_{\text{max}}$, the sampled signal's discrete-time Fourier transform exhibits \textit{aliasing}: frequency components above $f_s / 2$ are reflected back into the band $[0, f_s / 2]$ and are indistinguishable from lower-frequency components.

For a sinusoid at frequency $f_0$, the aliases appear at $|k f_s \pm f_0|$ for integer $k$.

\subsection*{A.3 Anti-Aliasing and Reconstruction}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Anti-aliasing:} Before sampling, apply a low-pass filter with cutoff at $f_s / 2$ to remove frequency components that would alias.
    \item \textbf{Reconstruction:} After digital processing, use a reconstruction filter (cutoff at $f_s / 2$) and DAC to return the signal to continuous time.
    \item \textbf{Ideal reconstruction filter:} The sinc function $\text{sinc}(x) = \sin(\pi x) / (\pi x)$ is the ideal interpolation kernel, achieving perfect reconstruction when the Nyquist condition is met.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{B.1 CD4066 Pin Configuration}

The CD4066 is a quad bilateral switch. For sampling, use switch A:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Pin 1: Common (input from signal source).
    \item Pin 2: Normally-open contact (output to capacitor).
    \item Pin 13: Control input (HIGH to close switch, LOW to open).
    \item Pins 14, 7, 8: Power (V$_{DD}$, V$_{SS}$, V$_{SS}$).
\end{itemize}

When Pin 13 is HIGH (control signal asserted), the switch closes (ON-resistance ~100 $\Omega$). When LOW, the switch opens (OFF-resistance > 10 M$\Omega$).

\subsection*{B.2 Arduino Timer1 Configuration for Sampling}

To generate a periodic sampling pulse, configure Timer1 (16-bit) in CTC (Clear Timer on Compare) mode:

\begin{verbatim}
// Configure Timer1 for 10 kHz sampling (16 MHz clock, prescaler 8)
// f_s = 16,000,000 / (8 * 200) = 10,000 Hz
TCCR1A = 0x00;           // Normal mode
TCCR1B = 0x0A;           // CTC mode, prescaler = 8
OCR1A = 199;             // Compare value (TOP = 199, counts 0-199 = 200 ticks)
TIMSK1 = 0x02;           // Enable compare A interrupt

ISR(TIMER1_COMPA_vect) {
    digitalWrite(11, HIGH);   // Trigger sample pulse
    delayMicroseconds(1);     // Hold for ~1 µs
    digitalWrite(11, LOW);    // Release
}
\end{verbatim}

Adjust OCR1A to change sampling rate: $f_s = 16,000,000 / (8 \times (\text{OCR1A} + 1))$.

\subsection*{B.3 Sample-and-Hold Timing}

The CD4066 control signal must be a narrow pulse (1--10 µs width) that occurs at regular intervals (period = $1/f_s$). The pulse width determines the effective sampling duration. Narrower pulses reduce droop but require faster slew-rate amplifiers to settle the capacitor voltage.
