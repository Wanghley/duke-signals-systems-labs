\section{Real-World Context: FIR Filters Drive Consumer Electronics}
%--------------------------------------------------------------

\noindent
You are a Junior DSP Engineer deployed across three product lines at your company. Each product depends on real-time FIR filters implemented in Simulink and auto-generated for embedded processors. Your role: design, validate, and ship these filters to millions of users worldwide.

\subsection{Program 1: Voice-Activated Smart Audio Devices (Consumer Electronics)}

Your company manufactures hearing aids, earbuds, and smart speakers with always-on microphone preprocessing. The challenge: raw microphone audio contains electrical hum (60 Hz), wind noise, background chatter, and rustling—all of which confuse downstream machine learning engines trained to recognize wake words.

\textbf{The engineering problem:} Voice intelligibility lives in the 85--4000 Hz band. Noise and artifacts above 4 kHz are useless for speech recognition and waste CPU cycles. But you cannot simply "turn off" the microphone above 4 kHz—audio sensors are analog continuous-time devices. You need an anti-aliasing FIR low-pass filter to remove high-frequency junk before downsampling and forwarding to the neural network.

\textbf{Requirements:} (1) Zero-phase response so speech timing is preserved ($< 5$ ms latency), (2) $\geq 60$ dB attenuation above 8 kHz to suppress ultrasonic clicks, (3) no discontinuities in the passband (0--4 kHz) to avoid audible artifacts. Your Simulink model auto-generates optimized ARM Cortex-M4 code. Production devices ship to 10 million users annually.

\subsection{Program 2: Cellular Modem Equalizers (Wireless Communications)}

Your company supplies the signal processing firmware for cellular base stations and handsets. The critical challenge: wireless channels are not ideal low-pass filters. They introduce frequency-selective fading (some frequencies attenuate more than others) and multipath distortion (echoes from building reflections).

\textbf{The engineering problem:} A signal transmitted at 2.5 GHz arrives at the receiver distorted. The channel's impulse response has multiple echoes. To recover the data, the receiver must apply an adaptive FIR equalizer—essentially the "inverse" of the channel—to flatten the frequency response and align multipath echoes.

\textbf{Requirements:} (1) Equalizer coefficients update in real-time (every 100 microseconds) to track channel changes, (2) 32-tap or 64-tap FIR filter running at 30.72 MHz sampling rate (LTE), (3) mean-squared-error between equalized output and reference falls below a strict threshold. Non-compliance breaks data throughput. Your Simulink-generated code runs on Qualcomm Snapdragon modems in 2 billion smartphones worldwide.

\subsection{Program 3: Medical EEG Monitoring for Epilepsy Detection (Biomedical Devices)}

Your company manufactures wearable EEG monitors for epilepsy patients. The device records brain electrical activity 24/7 and alerts patients to impending seizures.

\textbf{The engineering problem:} EEG signals are extraordinarily noisy. Muscle artifacts (EMG) from jaw clenching, eye blinks, and motion produce large-amplitude transients (1--100 Hz) that can mimic seizure-like activity. Electrical interference from power lines (60 Hz fundamental, harmonics at 120, 180, 240 Hz) also corrupts the recording. Meanwhile, true seizure activity is a high-amplitude, high-frequency burst (typically 3--25 Hz for temporal lobe seizures).

\textbf{Requirements:} (1) Bandpass FIR filter passing 1--40 Hz (seizure band) while rejecting power-line interference and muscle artifacts, (2) Group delay $< 100$ ms (regulatory requirement for alert generation speed), (3) Clinical sensitivity $\geq 85\%$ and specificity $\geq 80\%$ (meaning the filter/detector pipeline must miss fewer than 15\% of true seizures and falsely alarm on fewer than 20\% of non-seizure events). Your Simulink model auto-generates code for an embedded ARM processor. Regulatory approval (FDA 510(k)) depends on hardware validation showing the filter meets these specs.

\subsection{The Common Foundation: Model-Based Design with Simulink}

All three programs use the same workflow:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{MATLAB design:} Hand-calculate or use \texttt{fir1()} to compute impulse response coefficients $h[n]$ for your specification (cutoff frequency, stopband attenuation, passband ripple).
    \item \textbf{Simulink implementation:} Drag the Discrete FIR Filter block into your model, load coefficients from the MATLAB workspace, and simulate end-to-end performance.
    \item \textbf{Code generation:} Press ``Generate Code'' and auto-generate optimized C/C++ ready for ARM, DSP, or FPGA hardware.
    \item \textbf{Hardware validation:} Measure the empirical frequency response of the deployed code and compare against theory—this is how engineers certify designs for regulatory approval and high-volume manufacturing.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent
In this lab, you will design a prototype FIR filter, validate it with hardware measurements, and understand why the MATLAB/Simulink workflow is the industry standard for real-time DSP deployment.

\section{Core Concepts: FIR Filters and the Fourier Transform}
%--------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{The Ideal Low-Pass Filter and Its Impulse Response}

An ideal low-pass filter with cutoff frequency $\omega_c$ (normalized to rad/sample) has a frequency response that is 1 (unity gain) below $\omega_c$ and 0 (total attenuation) above $\omega_c$. The inverse Fourier transform of this ideal response yields the sinc function:

\begin{equation}
h_{\text{ideal}}[n] = \text{sinc}(n \omega_c / \pi) = \frac{\sin(n \omega_c)}{ \pi n},
\end{equation}

\noindent
with the convention that $h_{\text{ideal}}[0] = \omega_c / \pi$ (from L'Hôpital's rule).

\noindent
The sinc function is non-causal (extends to $n = -\infty$) and infinite-length. Real filters approximate it by:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Sampling:} Evaluate the sinc at discrete times $n = 0, \pm 1, \pm 2, \ldots$
    \item \textbf{Windowing:} Truncate to a finite length $N$ (e.g., $N = 11$ coefficients).
    \item \textbf{Centering:} Place the window symmetrically around $n = 0$ so the filter is nearly zero-phase.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Finite Impulse Response (FIR) Filters}

An FIR filter is defined by the convolution equation:

\begin{equation}
y[n] = \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} h[k] \, x[n-k] = h[n] * x[n],
\end{equation}

\noindent
where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $h[k]$ are the filter \textbf{coefficients} (or taps).
    \item $N$ is the \textbf{filter order} (total number of taps).
    \item $x[n]$ is the input signal.
    \item $y[n]$ is the filtered output.
    \item The asterisk $*$ denotes convolution.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
\textbf{Key properties:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Linear:} Scaling the input scales the output proportionally.
    \item \textbf{Time-invariant:} Delaying the input delays the output by the same amount.
    \item \textbf{Causal:} The output at time $n$ depends only on past and present inputs (no lookahead).
    \item \textbf{Stable:} If all coefficients are finite, the filter is always stable (bounded inputs produce bounded outputs).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Frequency Response of an FIR Filter}

The frequency response is the Discrete-Time Fourier Transform (DTFT) of the impulse response:

\begin{equation}
H(e^{j\omega}) = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} h[n] e^{-j\omega n},
\end{equation}

\noindent
where $\omega$ is the normalized angular frequency (rad/sample), and $\omega \in [0, \pi]$ corresponds to the Nyquist band. The magnitude response $|H(e^{j\omega})|$ tells you how much each frequency is attenuated or amplified.

\noindent
MATLAB's \texttt{freqz()} function computes this efficiently and plots the magnitude and phase responses.

\subsection{Convolution in the Frequency Domain}

The \textbf{convolution property} states that multiplication in the time domain is convolution in the frequency domain, and vice versa:

\begin{equation}
Y(e^{j\omega}) = H(e^{j\omega}) \cdot X(e^{j\omega}).
\end{equation}

\noindent
In plain English: the output spectrum is the product of the filter's frequency response and the input spectrum. High frequencies are attenuated if $|H(e^{j\omega})|$ is small at those frequencies.

\section{Fourier Transform Fundamentals}
%--------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Continuous-Time Fourier Transform (CTFT)}

The CTFT of a continuous-time signal $x(t)$ is defined as:

\begin{equation}
X(j\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t) e^{-j\omega t} \, dt,
\end{equation}

\noindent
and the inverse CTFT is:

\begin{equation}
x(t) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} X(j\omega) e^{j\omega t} \, d\omega.
\end{equation}

\noindent
The CTFT maps time-domain signals to frequency-domain representations, revealing the spectral content (which frequencies are present and at what amplitudes).

\subsection{The Ideal Low-Pass Filter}

An ideal low-pass filter has a rectangular frequency response:

\begin{equation}
H_{\text{ideal}}(j\omega) = \begin{cases}
1 & |\omega| < \omega_c \\
0 & |\omega| \ge \omega_c
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

\noindent
Applying the inverse CTFT yields the sinc impulse response:

\begin{equation}
h_{\text{ideal}}(t) = \frac{\omega_c}{\pi} \, \text{sinc}\left(\frac{\omega_c t}{\pi}\right).
\end{equation}

\noindent
This ideal response extends from $t = -\infty$ to $t = \infty$ and is thus non-realizable in practice. Real filters approximate this ideal by windowing and truncating the sinc function.

\section{Discrete-Time Fourier Transform and FIR Filters}

\subsection{Discrete-Time Fourier Transform (DTFT)}

For a discrete-time signal $x[n]$, the DTFT is:

\begin{equation}
X(e^{j\omega}) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} x[n] e^{-j\omega n},
\end{equation}

\noindent
where $\omega \in [0, 2\pi)$ is the normalized angular frequency. The DTFT is periodic with period $2\pi$.

\subsection{FIR Filter Design: Window Method}

The most straightforward design method:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Start with the ideal sinc impulse response.
    \item Truncate to $N$ coefficients centered at $n = 0$.
    \item Optionally apply a window function (rectangular, Hamming, Hann) to reduce side lobe artifacts.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent
MATLAB's \texttt{fir1()} implements this using a Hamming window by default, which provides good frequency selectivity and low ripple in both passband and stopband.

\section{Troubleshooting Guide}

\subsection{Pre-Lab Issues}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{fir1() not available:} Verify Signal Processing Toolbox is installed. Run \texttt{ver} in MATLAB to check.
    \item \textbf{Sinc values disagree with MATLAB:} Double-check the sinc definition. MATLAB uses $\text{sinc}(x) = \sin(\pi x) / (\pi x)$ with $\text{sinc}(0) = 1$.
    \item \textbf{Coefficients very different from MATLAB output:} Verify your normalized frequency is in [0, 1]. Compare with \texttt{fir1(10, omega\_c/pi)} in MATLAB.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Simulink Deployment Issues}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Arduino not recognized:} Check USB cable and ensure Arduino board shows in Device Manager (Windows) or \texttt{/dev/} (Mac/Linux). Try ``Build Hardware'' without deployment first.
    \item \textbf{Build fails with unknown symbol:} Ensure \texttt{h\_filter} variable is in the MATLAB base workspace. Run \texttt{assignin('base', 'h\_filter', h\_filter);} before deployment.
    \item \textbf{ADC reads all zeros:} Check that A0 is connected to the preamplifier output and powered. Verify Arduino can read analog voltages independently (e.g., with a simple sketch).
    \item \textbf{No output on Pin 9:} Verify the DAC output block is properly configured. Check Pin 9 with a multimeter (should see toggling PWM).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Measurement Issues}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Scope shows very noisy signal:} Verify oscilloscope probes are properly grounded. Use short, twisted probe leads. Increase averaging if available.
    \item \textbf{Measured attenuation is much less than expected:} Check that the correct coefficients were loaded into the FIR block. Re-deploy the model if coefficients changed.
    \item \textbf{Frequency response doesn't match theory:} ADC/DAC quantization and PWM timing jitter cause deviations. These are normal; document and explain in your analysis.
\end{itemize}
