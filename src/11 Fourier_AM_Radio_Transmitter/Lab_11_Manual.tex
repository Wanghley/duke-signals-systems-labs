\section{Real-World Context: Amplitude Modulation in Critical Communications}
%--------------------------------------------------------------

\noindent
You are a Junior Communications Hardware Engineer at a company supplying radio systems to three critical infrastructure sectors: aviation, maritime, and emergency services. Your expertise in AM modulation and hardware validation directly impacts safety and regulatory compliance across these domains. Each system relies on the same physical principle—multiplication (modulation) in time-domain becomes frequency-shifting in the Fourier domain—but with vastly different specifications and consequences for failure.

\subsection{Program 1: Aviation—Air Traffic Control and Emergency Locator Transmitters}

The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) mandates that commercial and military aircraft carry AM radio systems for navigation and emergencies. Your company designs both primary systems and backup Emergency Locator Transmitters (ELTs) that activate on impact.

\textbf{The challenge:} Modern digital systems offer higher data rates but fail catastrophically when signal-to-noise ratio drops below a threshold. Pilots in distress need human-intelligible voice communication—not error-corrected bit streams. AM degrades gracefully: as the signal weakens, the voice becomes scratchy but remains intelligible. This is why the FAA mandates AM as a primary and backup system for the next 20+ years.

\textbf{Your role:} Design the AM modulator hardware (using an AD633 four-quadrant multiplier) and validate that the modulation index (envelope depth) stays within FAA-approved ranges (typically 90--100\% for double-sideband AM). Physical proof: capture the modulated signal on an oscilloscope, compute the empirical spectrum via FFT, and show that sidebands are precisely at the expected frequencies with no spurious emissions. The modulated signal must meet FCC spectral mask requirements—no power spilling into adjacent channels.

\textbf{Regulatory requirement:} Demonstrate that your AD633 hardware modulator produces identical spectral content to the ideal Simulink mathematical model (deviation $< 2\%$ in sideband amplitude). Aviation equipment certification requires hardware-software correlation.

\subsection{Program 2: Maritime—Ship-to-Shore Communication and Distress Signaling}

The International Maritime Organization (IMO) requires all ocean-going vessels to carry Medium Frequency (MF) AM radio for ship-to-shore communication and distress alerting (MAYDAY, SOS). Even modern vessels with satellite systems maintain AM as a primary backup.

\textbf{The challenge:} Ships operate in extreme environments—saltwater corrosion, high-humidity antenna systems, lightning strikes, and electromagnetic interference from radar and power systems. MF AM radio (around 500 kHz to 2 MHz) propagates over long distances by ground wave and sky wave, often reaching stations even when the main antenna is damaged or the digital system has failed.

\textbf{Your role:} Design an AM transmitter front-end that can survive harsh marine environments and deliver reliable modulation despite component aging, thermal variations ($-20°$ to $+60°$ C), and power supply ripple. Your lab validates that the AD633 multiplier maintains stable modulation index across this temperature range.

\textbf{Regulatory requirement:} Prove modulation meets ITU-R recommendations for MF transmitters (modulation index 95--100\%, frequency stability within 50 ppm). The spectrum must show clean sidebands with no splatter into neighboring ship frequencies (spaced 3 kHz apart in the MF band).

\subsection{Program 3: Emergency Services—Emergency Alert System (EAS) Radio}

The FCC requires all broadcast radio and television stations to carry Emergency Alert System (EAS) signals—AM-modulated codes that alert the public to hurricanes, earthquakes, Amber Alerts, and national emergencies.

\textbf{The challenge:} EAS must work on cheap, ubiquitous AM radios in every home and car—millions of legacy receivers from the 1970s onward, many with poor selectivity and limited dynamic range. A weak modulator with harmonic distortion creates adjacent-channel interference that blocks reception of neighboring broadcasts.

\textbf{Your role:} Design a compact, low-cost AM modulator suitable for small-market broadcast stations and emergency radio transmitters. Your lab measures the Total Harmonic Distortion (THD) of the AD633 output when modulated with broadband audio (music, speech, alert tones) and proves THD stays below 5\% (FCC requirement for broadcast stations). You must show that your hardware FFT reveals a clean carrier and sidebands with no harmonic spurs that would interfere with adjacent channels.

\textbf{Regulatory requirement:} Demonstrate that the EAS alert signal modulation remains intelligible and activates receivers reliably even in the presence of moderate atmospheric noise and interference (SNR down to 10 dB).

\subsection{The Unifying Principle: Hardware Validation Against Mathematical Theory}

All three programs depend on a core engineering skill: \textbf{proving that physical hardware matches ideal mathematical models}.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Hand-derived predictions:} Use AM modulation theory to predict carrier frequency, sideband frequencies, and sideband amplitudes.
    \item \textbf{Hardware build:} Construct the AD633 multiplier circuit on a breadboard.
    \item \textbf{Oscilloscope observation:} Measure the modulated waveform's envelope and verify that the modulation index matches predictions.
    \item \textbf{FFT analysis:} Capture the modulated signal into MATLAB and compute its frequency spectrum. Overlay with ideal Simulink predictions.
    \item \textbf{Quantify accuracy:} Compare hardware spectrum to theory. Explain any deviations (component tolerance, nonlinearity, temperature drift).
\end{enumerate}

This workflow—theory → hardware → measurement → comparison → validation—is how modern engineers certify safety-critical systems for aviation, maritime, and emergency services.

\section{Core Concepts: Amplitude Modulation and Fourier Analysis}
%--------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{What Is Amplitude Modulation?}

Amplitude Modulation (AM) is the process of encoding an information-bearing signal (the \textbf{message} or \textbf{baseband} signal $m(t)$) onto a high-frequency oscillation (the \textbf{carrier} signal). The simplest form is:

\begin{equation}
y(t) = [A_c + m(t)] \cos(\omega_c t),
\label{eq:am_basic}
\end{equation}

\noindent
where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $m(t)$ is the baseband message signal (audio, for example).
    \item $A_c$ is the carrier amplitude.
    \item $\omega_c = 2 \pi f_c$ is the carrier angular frequency (radians/second).
    \item The bracketed term $[A_c + m(t)]$ is the \textbf{envelope}—the time-varying amplitude of the carrier wave.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
\textbf{Key insight:} The envelope directly follows the message signal. A listener tuned to frequency $f_c$ hears the audio because the envelope contains all the spectral content of $m(t)$.

\subsection{DSB-SC vs. DSB-AM: What's the Difference?}

There are two common AM variants:

\subsubsection*{Double-Sideband Suppressed-Carrier (DSB-SC)}

\begin{equation}
y_{\text{DSB-SC}}(t) = m(t) \cos(\omega_c t).
\label{eq:dsbsc}
\end{equation}

\noindent
Here, there is \textbf{no} DC offset. The modulated signal is purely the product of the message and the carrier. In the frequency domain, this produces \textit{two sidebands} at $f_c + f_m$ and $f_c - f_m$, with the \textbf{carrier suppressed} (zero energy at $f_c$).

\subsubsection*{Double-Sideband with Amplitude Modulation (DSB-AM or Standard AM)}

\begin{equation}
y_{\text{DSB-AM}}(t) = [A_c + m(t)] \cos(\omega_c t).
\label{eq:dsbam}
\end{equation}

\noindent
Here, a DC bias $A_c$ is added before multiplication. The modulated signal now contains:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The \textbf{carrier} component at frequency $f_c$ (with amplitude $A_c/2$).
    \item The \textbf{upper sideband} at $f_c + f_m$.
    \item The \textbf{lower sideband} at $f_c - f_m$.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
Standard AM broadcasts use this form because the carrier can be recovered by a simple amplitude detector (rectifier + low-pass filter), making receiver designs cheaper and simpler.

\subsection{Single-Tone Modulation: Hand-Derivable Spectra}

Suppose the message is a pure sinusoid: $m(t) = A_m \cos(\omega_m t)$.

\noindent
\textbf{For DSB-SC:}

\begin{equation}
y_{\text{DSB-SC}}(t) = A_m \cos(\omega_m t) \cos(\omega_c t) = \frac{A_m}{2}[\cos(\omega_c + \omega_m) t + \cos(\omega_c - \omega_m) t].
\end{equation}

\noindent
The frequency spectrum contains exactly two spectral lines:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Upper sideband:} $f_{\text{USB}} = f_c + f_m$ (amplitude $A_m/2$).
    \item \textbf{Lower sideband:} $f_{\text{LSB}} = f_c - f_m$ (amplitude $A_m/2$).
\end{itemize}

\noindent
\textbf{For DSB-AM:}

\begin{equation}
y_{\text{DSB-AM}}(t) = [A_c + A_m \cos(\omega_m t)] \cos(\omega_c t).
\end{equation}

\noindent
Expanding:

\begin{equation}
y_{\text{DSB-AM}}(t) = A_c \cos(\omega_c t) + \frac{A_m}{2}[\cos(\omega_c + \omega_m) t + \cos(\omega_c - \omega_m) t].
\end{equation}

\noindent
The frequency spectrum now contains \textit{three} spectral lines:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Carrier:} $f_c$ (amplitude $A_c$).
    \item \textbf{Upper sideband:} $f_{\text{USB}} = f_c + f_m$ (amplitude $A_m/2$).
    \item \textbf{Lower sideband:} $f_{\text{LSB}} = f_c - f_m$ (amplitude $A_m/2$).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Modulation Index}

For DSB-AM, the \textbf{modulation index} (or \textbf{depth of modulation}) is defined as:

\begin{equation}
m = \frac{A_m}{A_c},
\end{equation}

\noindent
where $A_m$ is the peak amplitude of the message signal and $A_c$ is the carrier DC offset.

\noindent
The modulation index characterizes the efficiency of power transmission:
\begin{itemize}
    \item If $m < 1$, the modulation is \textit{under-modulated}: the signal is not using full power.
    \item If $m = 1$, the modulation is \textit{critically modulated}: this is the maximum before distortion.
    \item If $m > 1$, the modulation is \textit{over-modulated}: the envelope collapses to zero, causing harmonic distortion.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
\textbf{Practical note:} You can extract $m$ directly from an oscilloscope display by measuring the maximum and minimum envelope voltages and applying:

\begin{equation}
m = \frac{V_{\max} - V_{\min}}{V_{\max} + V_{\min}}.
\end{equation}

\subsection*{AD633 Pinout and Ratings}

The AD633 is a classic four-quadrant analog multiplier. Key specifications:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pins:} X input, Y input, W (divider reference, usually tied to +10 V), and product output.
    \item \textbf{Supply:} Requires symmetric dual supply (+15 V, --15 V, GND).
    \item \textbf{Output range:} --5 V to +5 V.
    \item \textbf{Bandwidth:} Approximately 1 MHz (suitable for audio and VF modulation).
    \item \textbf{Equation:} $V_{\text{out}} = \frac{V_X \cdot V_Y}{10 \text{ V}}$ (the 10 V denominator is built into the chip).
\end{itemize}

\section{Safety and Critical Wiring Notes}
%--------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dual Power Supply:} The AD633 requires both +15 V and --15 V. Never apply power without \textit{verifying} both supply rails are present with a multimeter. Reversed polarity will destroy the chip instantly.
    \item \textbf{Input Ranges:} Keep all AD633 inputs (X, Y, W) within the rated range (typically --10 V to +10 V). Do not apply signals directly from the Arduino (0--5 V) without understanding the circuit behavior.
    \item \textbf{Output Loading:} The AD633 output is relatively high-impedance ($\sim$ 50--100 $\Omega$). Connect a 1 k$\Omega$ resistor to ground to terminate the output cleanly.
    \item \textbf{Oscilloscope Grounding:} Always connect oscilloscope probe grounds to the circuit ground. Floating probe grounds introduce noise and EMI.
    \item \textbf{Scope Probe Impedance:} Use 10× probe attenuation (not 1×) to avoid loading the AD633 output and introducing measurement artifacts.
\end{itemize}

\section{Amplitude Modulation and Communication Theory}
%--------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Historical Context: Why AM Still Matters}

Amplitude Modulation was invented by Reginald Fessenden in the early 1900s and remains the foundation of global emergency communications. Modern digital systems (FM, QAM, OFDM) offer higher data rates but fail catastrophically at low SNR. AM, by contrast:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Degrades gracefully: even with 50\% noise corruption, the message remains intelligible.
    \item Requires minimal circuitry: a simple diode rectifier can demodulate AM.
    \item Is robust to frequency drift and Doppler shift.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
This is why aviation, maritime, and military systems mandate AM for critical safety communications.

\subsection{The Convolution Theorem and Frequency Shifting}

Multiplication in the time domain corresponds to convolution in the frequency domain. If $m(t)$ has spectrum $M(f)$ and the carrier $\cos(2 \pi f_c t)$ has spectrum consisting of delta functions at $\pm f_c$, then the product $m(t) \cos(2 \pi f_c t)$ has spectrum:

\begin{equation}
Y(f) = M(f) * [\delta(f - f_c) + \delta(f + f_c)] / 2 = \frac{1}{2}[M(f - f_c) + M(f + f_c)].
\end{equation}

\noindent
This shifts the spectrum of $m(f)$ to both $f_c$ and $-f_c$, creating the sidebands. This is the mathematical foundation of all analog modulation.

\subsection{Power and Efficiency}

For DSB-AM with modulation index $m$, the total power is distributed among three components:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Carrier power:} $P_c = A_c^2 / 2$ (constant, carries no information).
    \item \textbf{Sideband power:} $P_{sb} = m^2 A_c^2 / 4$ (carries the message information).
\end{itemize}

\noindent
The \textbf{modulation efficiency} is:

\begin{equation}
\eta = \frac{P_{sb}}{P_c + P_{sb}} = \frac{m^2 / 4}{1 + m^2 / 2} \approx \frac{m^2}{2} \quad \text{(for small } m \text{)}.
\end{equation}

\noindent
For $m = 1$ (critical modulation), the efficiency is approximately 33\%—two-thirds of the power is wasted on the carrier. This is why more modern schemes (SSB, QAM) suppress the carrier entirely.

\section{The AD633 Analog Multiplier: Nonidealities}

\subsection{Real-World Effects}

The AD633 is a classic op-amp-based four-quadrant multiplier, but it has practical limitations:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Input offset voltage:} Typically $\pm 50$ mV, causing a small DC shift in the output.
    \item \textbf{Nonlinearity:} The transfer function is not perfectly proportional; higher-order terms create harmonic distortion.
    \item \textbf{Bandwidth:} Limited to about 1 MHz; higher frequency signals are attenuated.
    \item \textbf{Noise:} Output noise floor is roughly 10 mV RMS, introducing a noise floor in the spectrum.
    \item \textbf{Temperature drift:} Coefficients drift with temperature, causing frequency and amplitude shifts.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
These nonidealities are why the hardware spectrum will never perfectly match the theory—but good engineering is about understanding and tolerating these imperfections.

\section{FFT Spectral Analysis: Resolution and Leakage}

\subsection{Spectral Resolution}

The frequency resolution of an FFT is:

\begin{equation}
\Delta f = \frac{F_s}{N},
\end{equation}

\noindent
where $F_s$ is the sampling rate and $N$ is the FFT length. To resolve closely spaced sidebands (e.g., $f_c \pm f_m$ where $f_m$ is small), you need:

\begin{equation}
N > \frac{F_s}{\Delta f_{\text{desired}}}.
\end{equation}

\noindent
\textbf{Rule of thumb:} Collect at least 10--20 complete cycles of the message signal to achieve resolution adequate to distinguish the carrier and sidebands.

\subsection{Spectral Leakage and Windowing}

If your signal length is not an integer multiple of the message period, spectral energy ``leaks'' from sharp lines into adjacent frequency bins. This can be mitigated by applying a window function (Hamming, Hann) before the FFT. However, windowing widens the spectral lines, so there's a trade-off between leakage reduction and spectral resolution.

\section{Troubleshooting Guide}

\subsection{Pre-Lab and Hardware Issues}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{AD633 not responding:} Check dual power supply voltages with multimeter. Verify +15 V and --15 V are present at the chip. If one supply is missing, the chip will not function.
    \item \textbf{Output is DC:} The X and Y inputs may not be connected properly. Verify carrier is connected to X input and message to Y input. Use a multimeter to confirm signals are present at the inputs.
    \item \textbf{Output is very noisy:} Check for loose breadboard connections. Use shorter jumper wires. Verify the output termination resistor (1 k$\Omega$ to GND) is in place.
    \item \textbf{Envelope looks distorted:} You may be over-modulated ($m > 1$). Reduce the message amplitude or increase the DC bias.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Software and MATLAB Issues}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Serial port won't open:} Verify the Arduino is connected to the laptop via USB. Check Device Manager (Windows) or \texttt{/dev/} (Mac/Linux) to confirm the COM port. Update the MATLAB serial port name accordingly.
    \item \textbf{FFT shows only noise:} Check that you're capturing enough data (at least 5--10 seconds). Verify the Arduino ADC is sampling at the correct rate and the serial script is reading the data correctly.
    \item \textbf{Sidebands don't align with prediction:} Verify your carrier and message frequencies are set correctly. Double-check the pre-lab spectrum calculation. If there's a frequency offset, the Arduino may be drifting due to clock inaccuracy.
    \item \textbf{Simulink model won't compile:} Ensure all Simulink blocks are properly connected. Check that the message and carrier frequencies match your assigned values.
\end{itemize}
