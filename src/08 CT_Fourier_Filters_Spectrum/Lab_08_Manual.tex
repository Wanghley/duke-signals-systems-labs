\section{Real-World Context: Spectral Analysis Without Digital Overhead}
%--------------------------------------------------------------

\noindent
You are a Junior Sensor Systems Engineer deployed across three advanced sensing programs at your company. Each program deploys continuous-time analog filters to extract frequency information directly—avoiding the power and latency penalties of digital FFT processing on battery-powered devices. Your role: design and validate the filter banks that power these systems.

\subsection{Program 1: Predictive Maintenance for Rotating Equipment (Industrial IoT)}

Your company builds ultra-low-power wireless vibration sensors for factories. These sensors clamp onto motors, pumps, and bearing assemblies, monitoring for early signs of mechanical failure.

\textbf{The challenge:} Running a full FFT every 100 ms on a microcontroller draws 50--100 mA continuously. A coin-cell battery (200 mAh) dies in hours. Yet sensors must operate 5+ years in the field to justify installation costs.

\textbf{Your solution:} Design an analog front-end (AFE)—a bank of 4--6 continuous-time bandpass filters that listen to specific frequency bands 24/7 (drawing only microamps). Each filter monitors one "symptom" band: bearing wear (8--12 kHz), imbalance (1x and 2x line frequency), blade-pass frequency, etc. When a specific band spikes above a threshold, only then does the CPU wake to perform deeper analysis. Total power consumption: $< 50$ µA continuous. Regulatory requirement: achieve $\pm 10\%$ frequency accuracy and identify frequency components within 50 Hz resolution.

\subsection{Program 2: Power Grid Frequency Stabilization (Electrical Utilities)}

Your company supplies grid-monitoring equipment to utility companies. These devices detect electrical anomalies—harmonics, imbalances, and frequency drift—that herald equipment failures or cascading blackouts.

\textbf{The challenge:} Power grid fundamental frequency is 60 Hz (50 Hz internationally), but harmonics—integer multiples (120 Hz, 180 Hz, 240 Hz, etc.)—indicate nonlinear loads, transformer saturation, and power quality degradation. Utilities need real-time spectral monitoring at monitoring stations worldwide with limited IT infrastructure and no reliance on cloud processing.

\textbf{Your solution:} Design a continuous-time filter bank that simultaneously measures 60 Hz (fundamental), 120 Hz (2nd harmonic), 180 Hz (3rd harmonic), 240 Hz (4th harmonic), and beyond. Each filter's output is fed to a low-bandwidth ADC. No FFT required—just selective analog measurement. Regulatory requirement: $< 1$ Hz frequency error and harmonic amplitude accuracy $\pm 5\%$ to meet NEMA and IEC 61000 power quality standards. The entire AFE consumes less power than a single microcontroller FFT.

\subsection{Program 3: Wireless ECG Monitoring for Cardiac Patients (Biomedical)}

Your company manufactures wearable cardiac monitors for remote patient monitoring. These devices detect arrhythmias and alert clinicians to life-threatening conditions.

\textbf{The challenge:} ECG signals contain the heartbeat (0.5--5 Hz fundamental, with harmonics), electrical noise (60 Hz power line interference), and muscle artifacts (20--200 Hz). A patient wearing the device all day drains a battery-powered system quickly if continuous high-rate digital processing is running.

\textbf{Your solution:} Design a dual-band analog filter: a low-pass filter (cutoff 40 Hz) passes the ECG heartbeat, while a notch filter (60 Hz, high Q) rejects power-line interference without affecting the ECG. The output is sampled at just 250 Hz (vs. 10 kHz if a digital filter had to suppress interference). Total power: 20 µA AFE plus minimal ADC overhead. Clinical requirement: achieve $\geq 95\%$ sensitivity and specificity for arrhythmia detection, with latency $< 2$ seconds for alert generation.

\subsection{The Common Design Principle: Analog Spectral Analysis}

All three programs rely on the same core principle: \textbf{use continuous-time filters to measure spectral content directly, without expensive digital transformation}.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Battery-powered devices:} Analog AFE stays active 24/7 for ~5 years. Digital FFT would kill the battery in weeks.
    \item \textbf{Real-time event detection:} Analog thresholding (comparator) triggers CPU wake instantly—no digital latency.
    \item \textbf{Scalability:} Analog solutions do not require firmware updates. Ship thousands of devices with fixed frequency coverage.
    \item \textbf{Regulatory compliance:} Utilities and medical devices operate under strict standards (NEMA, IEC, FDA). Analog provides deterministic, auditable measurement.
\end{itemize}

\section{Analog Filters and Frequency Response}
%--------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Continuous-Time Filter Fundamentals}

A continuous-time (CT) filter is characterized by its \textbf{frequency response}:
\begin{equation}
H(j\omega) = |H(j\omega)| e^{j\angle H(j\omega)},
\end{equation}
where $\omega = 2\pi f$ is the angular frequency (rad/s).

For a bandpass filter, the magnitude response has a peak at the \textbf{center frequency} $\omega_c = 2\pi f_c$. The width of the peak (at half-power, or $-3$ dB) defines the \textbf{bandwidth}:
\begin{equation}
BW = f_{high} - f_{low} \quad \text{(Hz)}.
\end{equation}

The \textbf{quality factor} (Q) is the ratio of center frequency to bandwidth:
\begin{equation}
Q = \frac{f_c}{BW}.
\end{equation}

Higher Q $\Rightarrow$ narrower, more selective filter. Lower Q $\Rightarrow$ wider, less selective.

\subsection{Active Bandpass Filter Topology}

A simple \textbf{active bandpass filter} using an op-amp has the form:

\textbf{Transfer function:}
\begin{equation}
H(s) = \frac{G \omega_c s}{s^2 + \frac{\omega_c}{Q} s + \omega_c^2},
\end{equation}
where $s = j\omega$ is the complex frequency, $G$ is the low-frequency gain, $\omega_c = 2\pi f_c$, and $Q$ is the quality factor.

The magnitude response at the center frequency is:
\begin{equation}
|H(j\omega_c)| = G \cdot Q.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Parseval's Theorem: Time-Domain Energy = Frequency-Domain Energy}

For a periodic signal $x(t)$ with period $T$, the \textbf{average power} (or energy over one period) is:

\textbf{Time-domain calculation:}
\begin{equation}
E_{\text{time}} = \frac{1}{T} \int_0^T x^2(t) \, dt.
\label{eq:parseval_time}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Frequency-domain calculation:}
If $x(t) = \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \left[ a_k \cos(k\omega_0 t) + b_k \sin(k\omega_0 t) \right]$, then:
\begin{equation}
E_{\text{freq}} = \frac{a_0^2}{4} + \frac{1}{2} \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} (a_k^2 + b_k^2).
\label{eq:parseval_freq}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Parseval's Theorem states:}
\begin{equation}
E_{\text{time}} = E_{\text{freq}}.
\label{eq:parseval}
\end{equation}

In your lab, you will verify this by measuring energy in both domains and computing the percent error.

\subsection{Filter Design Equations}

For a standard \textbf{Sallen-Key (voltage-controlled voltage source) bandpass topology}, the component values are related to the desired center frequency and Q factor by:

\begin{align}
\omega_c &= 2\pi f_c = \frac{1}{R_1 R_2 C_1 C_2} \\
Q &= \frac{\pi f_c C_1 (R_1 + R_2)}{2}
\end{align}

If you assume \textbf{equal resistances} ($R_1 = R_2 = R$) and \textbf{equal capacitances} ($C_1 = C_2 = C$):

\begin{align}
\omega_c &= \frac{1}{RC} \quad \Rightarrow \quad RC = \frac{1}{\omega_c} = \frac{1}{2\pi f_c} \\
Q &= \frac{\pi f_c \cdot C \cdot 2R}{2} = \pi f_c RC = 1
\end{align}

So with equal components, you get $Q = 1$ (a moderately selective filter).

\subsection{System Architecture}

The lab's AFE consists of:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Arduino Signal Generator:} Outputs a ``mystery signal'' (a TA-assigned periodic waveform) on D11 via PWM.
    \item \textbf{Bandpass Filter Bank:} 3--4 op-amp active filters, each tuned to different center frequencies, all receiving the same input signal.
    \item \textbf{Peak Detector (or ADC direct):} Each filter's output is connected to an Arduino ADC pin to measure peak voltage (or RMS via averaging).
    \item \textbf{Oscilloscope Probe:} Measures the input waveform and at least one filter output to verify operation.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Bandpass Filter Circuit}

A standard \textbf{Sallen-Key non-inverting bandpass topology} uses one op-amp per stage:

\textbf{Key design elements:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Input signal goes to a high-pass RC stage (passes high frequencies, blocks DC).
    \item Output of high-pass drives the non-inverting input of the op-amp.
    \item Feedback network includes a low-pass RC stage (passes low frequencies in the feedback).
    \item The combination creates a bandpass response peaked at $f_c$.
\end{itemize}

On your breadboard:

\begin{enumerate}

\subsection*{A.1 Fourier Series and Energy}

For a periodic signal $x(t)$ with period $T$:

\textbf{Fourier Series representation:}
\begin{equation}
x(t) = \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \left[ a_k \cos(k\omega_0 t) + b_k \sin(k\omega_0 t) \right],
\end{equation}
where $\omega_0 = 2\pi / T$.

\textbf{Parseval's Theorem:}
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{T} \int_0^T x^2(t) \, dt = \frac{a_0^2}{4} + \frac{1}{2} \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} (a_k^2 + b_k^2).
\end{equation}

The left side is the average power in the time domain. The right side is the energy summed across all frequency components.

\subsection*{A.2 Frequency Response of Filters}

The magnitude response at the center frequency of a bandpass filter with gain $G$ and quality factor $Q$ is:
\begin{equation}
|H(j\omega_c)| = G \cdot Q.
\end{equation}

The half-power bandwidth (where $|H(j\omega)| = |H(j\omega_c)| / \sqrt{2}$) is:
\begin{equation}
BW = \frac{\omega_c}{Q}.
\end{equation}

\subsection*{B.1 Sallen-Key Bandpass Configuration}

The Sallen-Key non-inverting bandpass filter has the transfer function:

\begin{equation}
H(s) = \frac{G \omega_c s}{s^2 + \frac{\omega_c}{Q} s + \omega_c^2}.
\end{equation}

For equal-component design (R, C same for both stages):
\begin{itemize}
    \item Center frequency: $f_c = \frac{1}{2\pi RC}$
    \item Quality factor: $Q = 1$ (with equal R and C)
    \item DC gain: $G = 1$ (non-inverting unity-gain topology)
\end{itemize}

To increase Q, reduce the feedback capacitor:
\begin{equation}
C_{\text{feedback}} = \frac{C}{Q}.
\end{equation}

\subsection*{B.2 Op-Amp Power Supply}

Standard audio-grade op-amps (TL072, OPA2134, LM358) require:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Dual rail: $\pm 5V$ or $\pm 15V$ (recommended for best signal range)
    \item Bypass capacitors: 100 nF ceramic + 10 $\mu$F electrolytic across each power rail pair
    \item Common ground: All signal grounds, power grounds, and Arduino GND connected at one point (star grounding)
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{C.2 Parseval Energy Verification}

\begin{verbatim}
% Time-domain energy
E_time = mean(x_time.^2);  % average power for periodic signal

% Frequency-domain energy (from FFT)
X = fft(x_time);
N = length(x_time);
magnitude_spectrum = abs(X(1:N/2)) / (N/2);

% Energy = sum of squared magnitudes (Parseval for real signals)
E_freq = sum(magnitude_spectrum.^2);

% Normalize to compare
E_freq_normalized = E_freq / (N/2);

% Percent error
error = abs(E_time - E_freq_normalized) / E_time * 100;

fprintf('E_time: %.6f\n', E_time);
fprintf('E_freq: %.6f\n', E_freq_normalized);
fprintf('Error: %.2f %%\n', error);
\end{verbatim}

\section*{Appendix D: Troubleshooting Guide}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Appendix D: Troubleshooting Guide}
%--------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection*{Hardware Issues}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Op-amp outputs are flat (constant voltage).}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Verify power supply voltage at the op-amp pins (should be $\pm 5V$ or $\pm 15V$).
        \item Check that feedback resistors are connected correctly (compare schematic to breadboard).
        \item Try a different op-amp (the one you have may be faulty).
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Filter output has no signal or very weak signal.}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Verify the input signal (from Arduino PWM) is reaching the first filter stage. Use oscilloscope on the filter input.
        \item Check capacitor values: a wrong value (e.g., 100 nF instead of 100 pF) will shift center frequency by 1000x.
        \item Ensure the Arduino is actually outputting on D11: use \texttt{analogWrite(D11, 128)} to test, measure 2.5 V on an oscilloscope.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Filter peak is not at the designed center frequency.}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Tolerance on resistors and capacitors can shift the center frequency by 5--10\%. Trim by adjusting capacitor value.
        \item Measure actual R and C values with a multimeter; use the measured values in the design equation to predict actual $f_c$.
        \item If peak is much lower than expected, you likely have too much capacitance (e.g., parallel capacitors from previous wiring).
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{ADC readings from A0, A1, A2, A3 are constant (no variation).}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Verify that the Arduino is actually reading the correct pins (check the sketch configuration).
        \item Ensure filter outputs are connected to ADC pins before power-up.
        \item Use a multimeter to verify there is a changing DC voltage at each filter output.
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{MATLAB Analysis Issues}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{FFT shows very small magnitudes (< 0.001 V).}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Check the normalization: for one-sided FFT, use \texttt{magnitude = abs(X) / (N/2)}, not \texttt{abs(X) / N}.
        \item Ensure your input signal is in volts (not ADC counts or normalized to 0--1).
        \item If signal was generated as PWM (0--255), convert to voltage: \texttt{x\_volts = (x\_adc / 127.5 - 1) * 5}.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Parseval error is very large (> 30\%)}\\
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Spectral leakage: FFT assumes the signal is periodic within the window. If the capture length is not an integer number of periods, leakage spreads energy across bins.
        \item Window the signal before FFT: \texttt{x\_windowed = x\_time .* hann(length(x\_time))'} reduces leakage.
        \item ADC quantization: 10-bit ADC introduces noise. A cleaner analog signal yields better agreement.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Hardware and software spectra don't match.}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Ensure you're using the same mystery signal for both: hardware filters receive it, MATLAB FFT operates on the same raw capture.
        \item Confirm your filter center frequencies are correct (match your pre-lab calculations).
        \item If discrepancy is large at one frequency, that filter may be mistuned—remeasure with oscilloscope.
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{When to Ask for TA Help}

\begin{itemize}
    \item If no op-amp output is visible on oscilloscope after power-up (likely power or feedback wiring issue).
    \item If Parseval error exceeds 50\% (suggests fundamental measurement or data capture problem).
    \item If you're unable to match any of your filter center frequencies to design predictions (may indicate component labeling error).
\end{itemize}
