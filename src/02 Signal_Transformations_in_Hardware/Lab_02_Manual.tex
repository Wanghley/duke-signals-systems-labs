\section{Core Concepts: Continuous and Discrete Signal Transformations}
%--------------------------------------------------------------

Signal transformations are the fundamental algebraic operations by which the domain (time axis) and range (amplitude axis) of a signal are manipulated. Mastery of these operations is prerequisite to every subsequent topic in this course---convolution, system responses, Fourier analysis, and filtering---because each of those computational procedures can be decomposed into and understood through elementary transformations. In production signal processing systems (seismic, radar, communications), systematic errors in transformation implementation frequently escape detection until they compound across millions of samples and render the final output useless.

\subsection{Fundamental Continuous-Time Signal Transformations}

Let $x(t)$ denote an arbitrary continuous-time base signal. The three elementary independent-variable and dependent-variable transformations are:

\subsubsection{Time Shift (Delay and Advance)}
A time shift by $t_0$ seconds maps $x(t) \rightarrow y(t) = x(t - t_0)$. The signal retains its shape but is translated along the time axis. A \emph{positive} $t_0$ produces a \emph{delay} (the feature that was at $t=0$ now appears at $t = t_0 > 0$); a \emph{negative} $t_0$ produces an \emph{advance}:
\begin{equation}
    y(t) = x(t - t_0), \qquad t_0 \in \mathbb{R}.
\end{equation}
For a sinusoidal base signal $x(t) = A\cos(\omega_0 t)$, a time shift by $t_0$ introduces a phase offset $\phi_0 = \omega_0 t_0$:
\begin{equation}
    y(t) = A\cos\!\bigl(\omega_0(t-t_0)\bigr) = A\cos(\omega_0 t - \phi_0).
    \label{eq:timeshift_sine}
\end{equation}
Equation~(\ref{eq:timeshift_sine}) is fundamental: it demonstrates that \emph{time delay and phase shift are equivalent representations for sinusoidal signals}, a relationship that underpins Fourier-domain analysis later in the course.

\subsubsection{Amplitude Scaling}
Amplitude scaling multiplies the signal's range by a scalar $B \in \mathbb{R}$:
\begin{equation}
    y(t) = B \cdot x(t).
\end{equation}
When $|B| > 1$ the signal is \emph{amplified}; when $0 < |B| < 1$ it is \emph{attenuated}; when $B < 0$ the signal is simultaneously scaled and \emph{inverted} (reflected about the horizontal axis). In this laboratory a potentiometer physically implements this operation by acting as a programmable voltage divider whose wiper position sets:
\begin{equation}
    B_{\text{hw}} = \frac{V_{\text{wiper}}}{V_{\text{supply}}} = \frac{\text{analogRead(A1)}}{1023} \in [0, 1].
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Time Reversal}
Time reversal reflects the signal about the vertical axis by replacing $t$ with $-t$:
\begin{equation}
    y(t) = x(-t).
\end{equation}
For a causal signal (one that is identically zero for $t < 0$), time reversal produces a signal that is nonzero only for $t \leq 0$---a non-causal output. Exact continuous-time reversal is therefore physically non-realizable in real time; however, it is \emph{exactly} implementable on a finite-duration buffered waveform stored in memory, which is precisely how the Arduino LUT approach realizes it.

\subsubsection{Combined Transformations and Order of Operations}
Transformations can be composed. The general affine time transformation is:
\begin{equation}
    y(t) = B \cdot x(at - t_0),
    \label{eq:combined}
\end{equation}
where $B$ is the amplitude scale, $a$ is the time scale factor (compression if $|a|>1$, expansion if $|a|<1$, reversal if $a = -1$), and $t_0$ is the time shift applied \emph{after} time scaling. \textbf{Order of operations matters}: time scaling and shifting do not commute unless the argument is rewritten carefully. In this laboratory $a = \pm 1$ only (no compression or expansion), so the combined form reduces to:
\begin{equation}
    y(t) = B \cdot x(\pm\, t - t_0).
\end{equation}

\subsection{Discrete-Time Analogues and MATLAB Operators}

When the base signal is stored as a finite-length vector $\mathbf{x} = [x[0],\; x[1],\; \ldots,\; x[N-1]]$ of $N$ samples at sampling period $T_s$, the continuous-time transformations map to exact array operations:

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \caption{Continuous-Time Transformations and Their Discrete-Time MATLAB Counterparts}
    \label{tab:transforms}
    \begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{l l X}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Transformation} & \textbf{CT Expression} & \textbf{MATLAB Discrete Equivalent}\\
    \midrule
    Time shift ($k$ samples) & $x(t - kT_s)$ & \texttt{circshift(x, k)} \\
    Amplitude scale by $B$   & $B \cdot x(t)$ & \texttt{B * x} \\
    Time reversal            & $x(-t)$        & \texttt{fliplr(x)} \\
    Shift then scale         & $B \cdot x(t - kT_s)$ & \texttt{B * circshift(x, k)} \\
    Reversal then shift      & $x(-t - t_0)$  & \texttt{circshift(fliplr(x), k)} \\
    Combined                 & $B \cdot x(-t - t_0)$ & \texttt{B * circshift(fliplr(x), k)} \\
    \bottomrule
    \end{tabularx}
\end{table}

\noindent\textbf{Important:} \texttt{circshift} implements a \emph{circular} shift---samples that shift off one end of the array reappear at the other end. This is exact only when the signal is periodic with period $NT_s$. For non-periodic or transient signals, a boundary wrap-around artifact will appear; documenting, explaining, and proposing a mitigation for this artifact is part of the post-lab analysis.

\subsection{Exponential and Sinusoidal Signals}

The two canonical signal families used as base signals in this laboratory are:

\subsubsection{Real Exponential Signals}
\begin{equation}
    x(t) = A e^{\alpha t}, \qquad A,\, \alpha \in \mathbb{R}.
\end{equation}
The sign of $\alpha$ determines the qualitative behavior: $\alpha < 0$ gives a \emph{decaying} (stable) exponential; $\alpha > 0$ gives a \emph{growing} (unstable) one. The \emph{time constant} $\tau = 1/|\alpha|$ is the time required for the amplitude to fall to $e^{-1} \approx 36.8\%$ of its initial value (for a decaying exponential) or to grow to $e^{1} \approx 271.8\%$ (for a growing one). In hardware, $\alpha$ is set by the LUT pre-computation parameters assigned by your TA.

\subsubsection{Sinusoidal Signals}
\begin{equation}
    x(t) = A\cos(\omega_0 t + \phi), \qquad \omega_0 = 2\pi f_0.
\end{equation}
The three defining parameters are: amplitude $A$ (V), angular frequency $\omega_0$ (rad/s), and initial phase $\phi$ (rad). Note that a pure sinusoid is neither causal nor finite-energy---it is an idealized infinite-duration signal. In hardware it is always windowed to a finite duration equal to one LUT period.

\subsubsection{Damped Sinusoidal Signals}
The damped sinusoid combines the exponential envelope with sinusoidal oscillation and arises naturally as the free response of any underdamped second-order linear system:
\begin{equation}
    x(t) = A e^{-\alpha t}\cos(\omega_0 t + \phi), \qquad \alpha > 0.
    \label{eq:damped_sine}
\end{equation}
The time constant $\tau = 1/\alpha$ governs the envelope decay, while $\omega_0$ governs the oscillation rate. These two parameters are independently controllable in firmware, making this waveform particularly instructive for studying transformation effects.

\subsection{Unit Impulse and Unit Step Functions}

The unit impulse $\delta(t)$ and unit step $u(t)$ are the two foundational singularity functions in signals and systems theory. While not directly output by the Arduino in this laboratory, their mathematical properties underpin the interpretation of every hardware measurement.

\subsubsection{Unit Impulse (Dirac Delta)}
\begin{equation}
    \delta(t) \triangleq \begin{cases} \infty, & t = 0 \\ 0, & t \neq 0 \end{cases}, \qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \delta(t)\, dt = 1.
\end{equation}
The impulse is rigorously defined by its \emph{sifting property}: for any signal $x(t)$ continuous at $t = t_0$,
\begin{equation}
    \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t)\,\delta(t - t_0)\, dt = x(t_0).
    \label{eq:sifting}
\end{equation}
Its discrete-time counterpart, the Kronecker delta, is directly representable in memory:
\begin{equation}
    \delta[n] = \begin{cases} 1, & n = 0 \\ 0, & n \neq 0. \end{cases}
\end{equation}
In MATLAB, a Kronecker delta of length $N$ centered at sample $k$ is simply:
\begin{verbatim}
    d = zeros(1, N);  d(k+1) = 1;   % MATLAB indexing starts at 1
\end{verbatim}

\subsubsection{Unit Step Function}
\begin{equation}
    u(t) = \begin{cases} 1, & t \geq 0 \\ 0, & t < 0 \end{cases}, \qquad u(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{t} \delta(\tau)\, d\tau.
    \label{eq:step}
\end{equation}
The step function acts as a \emph{causal window}: multiplying any signal $x(t)$ by $u(t - t_0)$ zeroes it out for $t < t_0$, modeling the physical action of turning on a source at time $t_0$. Its discrete counterpart is:
\begin{equation}
    u[n] = \begin{cases} 1, & n \geq 0 \\ 0, & n < 0. \end{cases}
\end{equation}
The fundamental calculus relationship between the two singularity functions,
\begin{equation}
    \delta(t) = \frac{d}{dt}\,u(t) \qquad \text{and} \qquad u(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{t} \delta(\tau)\, d\tau,
    \label{eq:delta_step_relation}
\end{equation}
will recur throughout the course when analyzing LTI system impulse and step responses.
