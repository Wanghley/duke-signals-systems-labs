\section{Real-World Context: AM Demodulation Across Risk-Critical Systems}
%--------------------------------------------------------------

\noindent
You are a Junior Receiver Systems Engineer at a company designing communications equipment for three distinct markets: emergency response, military/defense, and consumer broadcast. Each domain requires AM demodulation, but the choice between envelope detection (simple, passive) versus synchronous demodulation (complex, active) reflects fundamentally different risk tolerances and operational constraints.

\subsection{Program 1: Search-and-Rescue (SAR) Radio Systems}

Your company supplies portable receivers to SAR teams that search for downed aircraft, shipwreck survivors, and lost hikers. These receivers must be rugged, require zero maintenance, and work in extreme conditions (ice, saltwater spray, high altitude, zero-degree temperatures).

\textbf{The challenge:} SAR signals arrive weak and noisy. Pilots may transmit distress calls with damaged radios. The receiver must extract every bit of information from a marginal signal in order for rescuers to locate survivors within the critical 72-hour window.

\textbf{Your solution:} An envelope detector—a single diode plus RC network. Advantages: (1) zero power consumption (passive), (2) zero calibration (works ``out of the box'' even after 10 years in storage), (3) proven reliability (used in emergency beacons since the 1950s). Disadvantages: harmonic distortion ($< 10\%$ THD acceptable for emergency voice), limited audio fidelity. RC time constant must be hand-tuned for the voice frequency range (300--3000 Hz); too slow and the signal distorts, too fast and ripple is audible.

\textbf{Regulatory requirement:} Prove that your envelope detector recovers intelligible voice from a signal with SNR as low as $-5$ dB (more noise than signal). Clinical listening tests show $> 90\%$ word recognition under these conditions.

\subsection{Program 2: Military Signal Intelligence (SIGINT) and Secure Communications}

Your company supplies receiver systems to military intelligence agencies. These systems intercept and analyze enemy broadcasts, monitor secure channels, and support encrypted communications networks.

\textbf{The challenge:} Modern secure communications often use Double-Sideband Suppressed-Carrier (DSB-SC) modulation—which omits the carrier frequency entirely. An envelope detector cannot recover DSB-SC signals (the envelope is constant). Only synchronous demodulation works: multiply the received signal by a recovered carrier and low-pass filter the product.

\textbf{Your solution:} A synchronous demodulator with a phase-locked loop (PLL) that tracks the transmitter's carrier frequency. Advantages: (1) recovers DSB-SC and other secure modulations, (2) achieves theoretical maximum signal-to-noise ratio (5.7 dB SNR improvement over envelope detection in white noise), (3) enables frequency-hopping and anti-jam reception when paired with agile frequency tracking. Disadvantages: phase lock loss is catastrophic ($90°$ phase error yields zero output even with a strong signal); power consumption is significant.

\textbf{Critical requirement:} Demonstrate that your PLL maintains phase lock on signals down to 0 dB SNR. Prove that a phase-mismatch error of $30°$ reduces the output amplitude by only $\cos(30°) = 86.6\%$ (acceptable), but $90°$ mismatch yields zero output (unacceptable, requires automatic reacquisition).

\subsection{Program 3: Consumer Broadcast AM Radio and Emergency Alert Systems}

Your company manufactures low-cost AM radio receivers for consumer and emergency markets. These receivers must work reliably, require minimal manufacturing cost, and serve both legacy analog broadcasts and modern Emergency Alert System (EAS) signals.

\textbf{The challenge:} Millions of older AM radios use passive envelope detectors and simple diode circuits. These devices work well enough for high-SNR local broadcasts but struggle with distant stations and weak signals. Yet, emergency alert systems depend on these same cheap radios reaching every home and vehicle during natural disasters.

\textbf{Your solution:} A hybrid approach: envelope detector as the primary demodulation stage (cost $< \$0.10$), augmented with optional active audio amplification and filtering. This achieves sufficient performance for emergency alerts while maintaining the reliability and simplicity of the envelope detector. For consumers wanting better audio quality, optional synchronous demodulation is available in premium models (cost $< \$3$ additional), where DSB-SC reception capability justifies the investment.

\textbf{Requirement:} Prove that an inexpensive envelope detector recovers intelligible emergency alerts from weak, fading signals (SNR 0--10 dB) across the standard AM broadcast band (530--1700 kHz). Show that premium synchronous models achieve $> 20$ dB SNR improvement for local stations while maintaining backward compatibility with legacy broadcasts.

\subsection{The Unifying Engineering Decision: Envelope vs. Synchronous}

The choice between these two demodulation methods is not about which is ``better''—it's about matching the technology to the use case:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Envelope Detection:} Robustness, simplicity, zero maintenance, zero power. Use when: emergency signals, cheap mass-market products, extreme environments, 50+ year operational life.
    \item \textbf{Synchronous Demodulation:} Optimal SNR, frequency flexibility, secure modulations, high fidelity. Use when: military intelligence, encrypted comms, premium audio, continuous power available.
\end{itemize}

\section{Core Concepts: Demodulation Methods and Phase Recovery}
\label{sec:concepts}
%--------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Envelope Detection: The Diode Demodulator}

The simplest AM receiver uses a \textbf{diode envelope detector}—a nonlinear circuit that rectifies the modulated signal and low-pass filters it to recover the baseband audio.

\subsubsection*{Circuit Operation}

\begin{equation}
\text{Diode } + \text{ RC circuit} \quad \Rightarrow \quad V_{\text{out}}(t) \approx |m(t)|,
\end{equation}

\noindent
where $m(t)$ is the original message signal. The diode conducts only when the input is positive, charging the capacitor. The RC network then discharges slowly, creating a low-frequency output that approximates the envelope.

\subsubsection*{Design Trade-Offs: The Time Constant}

The RC time constant $\tau = RC$ determines the circuit's response:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{If $\tau$ is too small} (e.g., $\tau = 1$ $\mu$s for a 1 kHz message): The capacitor discharges too quickly, and the output cannot follow the slowly varying envelope. Result: \textbf{Excessive ripple}—high-frequency oscillations appear in the demodulated signal.

    \item \textbf{If $\tau$ is too large} (e.g., $\tau = 100$ ms for a 1 kHz message): The capacitor discharges too slowly, and the envelope detector cannot track rapid changes in the message amplitude. Result: \textbf{Diagonal clipping}—the output lags the input, and peaks are clipped off or smoothed away, causing audio distortion.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
A rule of thumb for choosing $\tau$ is:

\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{10 f_m} \lesssim \tau \lesssim \frac{1}{f_c},
\end{equation}

\noindent
where $f_m$ is the highest audio frequency and $f_c$ is the carrier frequency. This ensures the RC circuit responds faster than the message but slower than the carrier.

\subsection{Synchronous Demodulation: The Coherent Receiver}

In \textbf{synchronous demodulation}, the receiver multiplies the received modulated signal by a phase-coherent local oscillator (LO) to downconvert the signal back to baseband.

\subsubsection*{The Mathematical Principle}

Suppose the received signal is:

\begin{equation}
y_{\text{rx}}(t) = [A_c + m(t)] \cos(\omega_c t).
\end{equation}

\noindent
If the receiver's LO is \textit{perfectly phase-locked}—i.e., $\text{LO}(t) = \cos(\omega_c t)$—then:

\begin{equation}
y_{\text{demod}}(t) = y_{\text{rx}}(t) \cdot \text{LO}(t) = [A_c + m(t)] \cos^2(\omega_c t).
\end{equation}

\noindent
Using the identity $\cos^2(\theta) = \frac{1}{2}[1 + \cos(2\theta)]$:

\begin{equation}
y_{\text{demod}}(t) = \frac{1}{2}[A_c + m(t)] \left[1 + \cos(2 \omega_c t)\right].
\end{equation}

\noindent
A low-pass filter removes the high-frequency $\cos(2 \omega_c t)$ term, leaving:

\begin{equation}
y_{\text{audio}}(t) = \frac{1}{2}[A_c + m(t)] \approx m(t) \quad \text{(after removing the DC term)}.
\end{equation}

\noindent
\textbf{Key insight:} Synchronous demodulation recovers the message perfectly, with minimal distortion.

\subsubsection*{Phase Mismatch: The Catastrophic Effect}

In reality, the receiver's LO may have a phase error:

\begin{equation}
\text{LO}(t) = \cos(\omega_c t + \Delta \phi),
\end{equation}

\noindent
where $\Delta \phi$ is the phase mismatch. Then:

\begin{equation}
y_{\text{demod}}(t) = [A_c + m(t)] \cos(\omega_c t) \cos(\omega_c t + \Delta \phi).
\end{equation}

\noindent
Using the product-to-sum identity:

\begin{equation}
\cos(A) \cos(B) = \frac{1}{2}[\cos(A-B) + \cos(A+B)].
\end{equation}

\noindent
With $A = \omega_c t$ and $B = \omega_c t + \Delta \phi$:

\begin{equation}
\cos(\omega_c t) \cos(\omega_c t + \Delta \phi) = \frac{1}{2}[\cos(-\Delta \phi) + \cos(2\omega_c t + \Delta \phi)] = \frac{1}{2}[\cos(\Delta \phi) + \cos(2\omega_c t + \Delta \phi)].
\end{equation}

\noindent
After low-pass filtering to remove the $2\omega_c$ term:

\begin{equation}
y_{\text{audio}}(t) = \frac{1}{2} \cos(\Delta \phi) \cdot [A_c + m(t)].
\end{equation}

\noindent
\textbf{Critical result:} The output amplitude is \textbf{scaled by} $\cos(\Delta \phi)$!

\begin{itemize}
    \item If $\Delta \phi = 0^\circ$: $\cos(0) = 1$ → Full output amplitude (perfect phase lock).
    \item If $\Delta \phi = 45^\circ$: $\cos(45^\circ) = 0.707$ → 70.7\% of full amplitude (--3 dB loss).
    \item If $\Delta \phi = 90^\circ$: $\cos(90^\circ) = 0$ → \textbf{Zero output} (signal completely lost!).
\end{itemize}

\noindent
This is why military receivers invest heavily in phase-locked loops (PLLs)—the cost of a 90-degree phase slip is losing the entire signal.

\section{Safety and Critical Wiring Notes}
%--------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Common Ground:} The Lab 12 receiver and Lab 11 transmitter \textbf{must share a common ground}. Floating grounds will cause ground loops, noise, and signal loss. Verify with the TA that both grounds are connected before powering up any equipment.
    \item \textbf{Input Protection:} If the Lab 11 team accidentally sends a high-voltage signal, it could damage the oscilloscope or Arduino ADC. Use a 10:1 attenuator probe on the oscilloscope and limit the input range with a 1 k$\Omega$ resistor in series with the receiver input.
    \item \textbf{Diode Polarity:} The 1N4148 diode has a striped end (cathode). Orient it correctly: the cathode connects to the RC network and output, the anode connects to the input signal.
    \item \textbf{Oscilloscope Probing:} Always use 10× probe attenuation to avoid loading the circuit. Connect probe grounds to the circuit ground; floating probe grounds cause noise coupling.
\end{itemize}

\section{Envelope Detection Theory and Design Details}
%--------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Diode Rectification}

The ideal diode has a forward voltage drop $V_F \approx 0.6$ V (for silicon). When the input signal $v_{\text{in}}$ exceeds $V_F$, the diode conducts and charges the capacitor. When the input falls below $V_F$, the diode blocks and the capacitor discharges through the resistor.

\noindent
The result is a voltage that approximately follows the envelope $|m(t)|$ of the modulated signal.

\subsection{Ripple and Clipping Analysis}

\subsubsection*{Ripple (Excessive $f_c / RC$)}

If the RC time constant is too small, the capacitor discharges too quickly between the peaks of the carrier cycles. The output voltage ripples at a frequency approximately equal to $f_c$. This ripple appears in the demodulated audio as high-frequency noise.

\noindent
Ripple magnitude (peak-to-peak):

\begin{equation}
V_{\text{ripple}} \approx \frac{I_{\text{discharge}} \cdot T_c}{C} = \frac{V_{\text{out}}}{R \cdot C} \cdot \frac{1}{f_c},
\end{equation}

\noindent
where $T_c = 1/f_c$ is the carrier period.

\subsubsection*{Clipping (Excessive $\tau$ relative to $f_m$)}

If the RC time constant is too large, the capacitor cannot discharge fast enough to track the message envelope. The output lags the input, and rapid changes (like audio transients) are clipped off or smoothed away. This causes harmonic distortion.

\subsection{Optimal Time Constant Selection}

A practical design rule balances ripple and clipping:

\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{10 f_m} \lesssim \tau = RC \lesssim \frac{1}{2 f_c}.
\end{equation}

\noindent
For an audio system with $f_m = 5$ kHz and $f_c = 100$ kHz:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Lower bound: $\tau > 20$ $\mu$s (to limit clipping).
    \item Upper bound: $\tau < 5$ $\mu$s (to limit ripple).
\end{itemize}

\noindent
Wait—these bounds are contradictory! This illustrates the fundamental trade-off: you cannot simultaneously eliminate ripple and clipping with a passive RC detector. A practical compromise is to aim for the geometric mean: $\tau \approx \sqrt{10 f_m \cdot 1/(2 f_c)}$.

\section{Synchronous Demodulation and Phase-Locked Loops}

\subsection{The Phase-Locked Loop (PLL)}

A phase-locked loop is a closed-loop feedback system that forces the receiver's local oscillator to match the phase and frequency of the incoming carrier. The PLL continuously measures the phase error between the incoming carrier and the LO, and adjusts the LO frequency and phase to minimize that error.

\noindent
\textbf{Key advantage:} The PLL can track frequency drift (Doppler shift, oscillator aging) and maintain phase coherence even in noisy conditions.

\noindent
\textbf{Trade-off:} PLLs require continuous power and can be slow to lock (lock time = several message periods). In SAR applications where battery life is critical, the simpler envelope detector remains preferred despite lower audio quality.

\subsection{Costas Loop and Quadrature Demodulation}

For DSB-SC signals (where the carrier is suppressed), the receiver cannot directly recover the carrier frequency by filtering the received signal. Instead, it uses a \textbf{Costas loop}, which:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Splits the received signal into two paths (I and Q components).
    \item Multiplies each by the LO (90 degrees apart).
    \item Uses a phase detector to measure the phase error.
    \item Feeds the error back to adjust the LO.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent
The Costas loop can recover the carrier phase from a DSB-SC signal with suppressed carrier, which a simple PLL cannot do.

%% Generate received signal
t = (0:1/Fs:T-1/Fs)';
m = A_m * cos(2*pi*f_m * t);  % Message
c = cos(2*pi*f_c * t);  % Carrier

%% Method 1: Envelope Detection (via Hilbert transform approximation)
% For simplicity, use MATLAB's envelope detection
[envelope, ~] = envelope(y_rx, round(Fs/(2*f_c)), 'analytic');

%% Method 2: Synchronous Demodulation at Delta_phi = 0
lo_ideal = cos(2*pi*f_c * t);  % Ideal LO
y_sync_0 = 2 * y_rx .* lo_ideal;
[b, a] = butter(4, 2*f_m / Fs);  % LPF at ~2*f_m

%% Method 2b: Synchronous Demodulation at Delta_phi = 45 deg

\section{Troubleshooting Guide}

\subsection{Cross-Bench Communication Issues}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{No signal from Lab 11:} Verify the shielded wire is connected to the correct pin on their AD633 output. Check that both grounds are connected. Confirm with the Lab 11 team that they are running and transmitting.
    \item \textbf{Signal is very noisy:} Shorten the shielded cable if possible. Move away from other equipment (power supplies, other transmitters). Add a 0.1 $\mu$F capacitor across the receiver input to ground for EMI filtering.
    \item \textbf{Intermittent signal loss:} Check for loose breadboard connections. Verify the ground connection between benches is secure. Ask Lab 11 if their transmitter is stable.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Envelope Detector Issues}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{No output or very small output:} Check diode polarity. The cathode (striped end) must connect to the RC network. Verify the diode is not burned out (test with multimeter in diode mode).
    \item \textbf{Excessive ripple:} Increase the capacitor value C to increase $\tau = RC$. Start by doubling C.
    \item \textbf{Output is clipped or distorted:} Decrease the capacitor value C (or increase R) to decrease $\tau$. Start by halving C.
    \item \textbf{Output cannot follow the message:} Your $\tau$ is too large. The message frequency is changing too fast for the RC network to track. Reduce $\tau$ by a factor of 2--5.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Simulink and MATLAB Issues}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Simulink model won't run:} Verify all blocks are connected. Check that the phase-mismatch parameter is a slider or settable variable (not a constant).
    \item \textbf{Filtered output is distorted:} The low-pass filter cutoff frequency may be too low. Verify the filter cutoff is $\geq 2 f_m$ to avoid attenuating the message.
    \item \textbf{Phase sweep results don't match prediction:} Double-check that you are varying the phase correctly (in radians, not degrees, if necessary). Verify the $\cos(\Delta \phi)$ scaling is applied after low-pass filtering.
\end{itemize}
