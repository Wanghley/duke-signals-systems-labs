\section{Real-World Context: Cross-Correlation for Embedded Systems}
%--------------------------------------------------------------

\noindent
You are a Junior Engineer at the Advanced Systems division of an aerospace, medical device, or robotics company. Your role is to design embedded signal processing systems that extract time delays from noisy acoustic or electromagnetic signals using cross-correlation. The challenge: computing power is limited, latency is critical, and every milliwatt of energy matters. In this section, we explore three real-world programs where your lab skills directly impact production systems.

\subsection{Program 1: Stratospheric Intelligence Platforms (Aerospace)}

Your company is designing the avionics system for a solar-powered autonomous aircraft operating at 70,000 feet. The platform collects imagery and atmospheric data over contested regions where GPS is spoofable and barometric altimeters drift with weather.

\textbf{The problem:} The aircraft must maintain stable cruise altitude through wind gusts and density changes. How do you measure altitude reliably?

\textbf{Your solution:} Acoustic ranging via cross-correlation. The aircraft transmits a brief ultrasonic chirp downward and listens for the echo from the ground. The time-of-flight equation gives distance:
\begin{equation}
d = \frac{v_{\text{sound}} \cdot \Delta t}{2}.
\end{equation}

But the received echo is buried in noise from wind, ground reflections, and receiver noise. Cross-correlation acts as a matched filter—it extracts the time delay with precision even in 5 dB SNR conditions. Your embedded implementation must run on a 16-bit microcontroller with 2 KB RAM and deliver the altitude estimate within 100 ms (critical for autopilot stability).

\subsection{Program 2: Prenatal Ultrasound Diagnosis (Medical Devices)}

Your company manufactures portable ultrasound systems for fetal monitoring in low-resource clinics. These devices must be battery-powered, operate in real-time, and deliver clinically accurate measurements with no connectivity.

\textbf{The problem:} Fetal position and growth require precise distance measurements from the transducer to anatomical landmarks (spine, head). Traditional systems rely on on-board GPU processing, but field deployments need embedded-only solutions.

\textbf{Your solution:} Correlation-based time-delay estimation. The ultrasound transducer transmits a pulse, and the received echoes from tissue boundaries are cross-correlated with a template waveform. The peak correlation lag directly gives distance-to-landmark. Your embedded system must achieve $<0.5$ cm accuracy on fetal measurements (regulatory requirement) while running on a low-power ARM Cortex-M4 with 256 KB RAM.

\subsection{Program 3: Autonomous Underwater Vehicle Sonar (Robotics)}

Your company develops AUVs for deep-sea mineral surveys. The vehicles operate autonomously in darkness with no GPS, relying solely on acoustic sensing for navigation and obstacle avoidance.

\textbf{The problem:} The vehicle must detect seafloor distance, identify geological features, and avoid subsea cables and equipment—all in real-time with latencies $< 50$ ms.

\textbf{Your solution:} Sonar via cross-correlation. The vehicle's forward-looking sonar transmits a chirp and cross-correlates the received echoes with the transmitted waveform. Multiple receiver elements enable beam-forming and multi-target detection. The correlation must run on an embedded Linux computer with strict power budgets (the vehicle operates 8-hour missions on battery).

\subsection{The Common Challenge: Edge vs. Cloud Processing Trade-Offs}

All three programs face the same critical decision: **where should signal processing happen?**

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cloud/Ground Station:} Raw ADC data streams to powerful computers (MATLAB servers, GPUs, cloud infrastructure). Algorithms are sophisticated (FFT-based detection, adaptive filtering, beamforming). \textbf{Advantage:} high accuracy and flexibility. \textbf{Disadvantage:} high latency (unacceptable for flight control or obstacle avoidance), high bandwidth (expensive satellite uplinks or sonar data links), high power (satellite modems drain batteries).

    \item \textbf{Edge/On-Board:} Embedded computer (Arduino, ARM Cortex, microcontroller) computes time delay on-the-fly. \textbf{Advantage:} immediate decisions (critical for autonomy), low power, low bandwidth, robust to communication dropouts. \textbf{Disadvantage:} limited compute means simpler algorithms, lower precision, quantization errors, potential accuracy loss.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
Your lab directly simulates this trade-off:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Simulate the ground station:} Use MATLAB's optimized \texttt{xcorr()} function with full-precision floating-point arithmetic and signal normalization. This achieves the best possible accuracy—representative of a cloud-based system.
    \item \textbf{Simulate the edge device:} Implement cross-correlation on the Arduino with integer arithmetic, limited window size, and resource constraints. This reveals real-world accuracy loss.
    \item \textbf{Analyze the trade-off:} Quantify the accuracy penalty of edge processing. For each real application, decide: can I tolerate the accuracy loss, or must I pay the latency/power/bandwidth cost to use the cloud?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Physics and Mathematics Background}
%--------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Sound Propagation and Time-of-Flight}

Sound travels through air at a speed that depends on temperature and pressure. Under standard laboratory conditions (room temperature, sea level), the speed of sound is approximately:
\begin{equation}
v_{\text{sound}} \approx 343 \text{ m/s}.
\end{equation}

When an acoustic pulse travels from a transmitter to a target (the floor, a wall, an object) and back to a receiver, the total distance traveled is $2d$ (down and back up). If the round-trip takes time $\Delta t$, then:
\begin{equation}
2d = v_{\text{sound}} \cdot \Delta t \quad \Rightarrow \quad d = \frac{v_{\text{sound}} \cdot \Delta t}{2}.
\label{eq:tof}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Example:} If the round-trip time is $\Delta t = 0.6$ ms, then:
\begin{equation}
d = \frac{343 \text{ m/s} \times 0.6 \times 10^{-3} \text{ s}}{2} = 0.1029 \text{ m} \approx 10.3 \text{ cm}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Cross-Correlation: The Mathematical Foundation}

\textbf{Problem:} You transmit a known signal $x[n]$ (a chirp), and receive a noisy echo $y[n]$. How do you reliably detect when the echo arrives?

\textbf{Solution:} Compute the cross-correlation between the transmitted and received signals:
\begin{equation}
R_{xy}[k] = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n] \cdot y[n+k],
\label{eq:xcorr}
\end{equation}
where $k$ is the lag (time shift in samples).

\textbf{Interpretation:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item $R_{xy}[k]$ measures how well the transmitted signal $x[n]$ aligns with the received signal $y[n]$ when shifted by $k$ samples.
    \item When $k$ equals the true delay, the alignment is best (the transmit chirp "matches" the echo), so $R_{xy}[k]$ reaches a maximum.
    \item The sample index $k_{\text{peak}}$ of this maximum directly tells us the time delay: $\Delta t = k_{\text{peak}} / f_s$, where $f_s$ is the sampling rate.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
\textbf{Why does this work?} The echo is a delayed, attenuated, and noisy replica of the transmitted signal. Cross-correlation is a matched filter—it extracts the signal of interest from noise and precisely locates its arrival time.

\subsection{Discrete-Time Implementation}

In the lab, the Arduino and MATLAB both work with discrete-time samples:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The transmitted signal is stored as an array $x[0], x[1], \ldots, x[N_x - 1]$.
    \item The received signal is captured as an array $y[0], y[1], \ldots, y[N_y - 1]$.
    \item The cross-correlation is computed for lags $k = 0, 1, 2, \ldots, N_y - N_x$ (or more, depending on the implementation).
    \item The peak lag $k_{\text{peak}}$ is found by searching for the maximum value in $R_{xy}[k]$.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
MATLAB's \texttt{xcorr()} function is optimized for speed and accuracy. The Arduino implementation will be simpler but slower, with fewer samples and lower precision.

\section{Safety and Wiring Notes}
%--------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Acoustic safety:} Ultrasonic transmitters operating at 40 kHz are inaudible to human ears but can be uncomfortable at close range. Avoid placing your ear directly in front of the transmitter.
    \item \textbf{Power supply:} The transmitter driver may draw up to 100 mA. Ensure Arduino power supply is adequate (typical USB supplies 500 mA).
    \item \textbf{Coupling capacitor:} A 10 µF capacitor may be needed between the function generator and the transmitter to block DC and pass the AC signal.
    \item \textbf{Receiver amplification:} The raw receiver output is weak (millivolts). Use a pre-amplified receiver board, or amplify with an op-amp circuit.
    \item \textbf{ADC input protection:} Keep A0 within the 0--5 V range at all times.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Transducer Basics}

An ultrasonic transducer system consists of:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Transmitter:} A piezoelectric element that vibrates at ~40 kHz, generating sound waves into the air.
    \item \textbf{Receiver:} Another piezoelectric element that picks up the echo. The received signal is very weak (millivolts) and noisy.
    \item \textbf{Pre-amplifier (optional):} An op-amp circuit that amplifies the received signal to usable levels (0--5 V for the ADC).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Wiring Configuration}

\textbf{Transmitter Drive:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Arduino pin D9 or D10 (PWM-capable) generates a ~40 kHz square wave or sinusoid.
    \item This signal is AC-coupled (through a 10 µF capacitor) to the transmitter element.
    \item The transmitter radiates acoustic energy downward (toward the target).
\end{itemize}

\noindent
\textbf{Receiver Path:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The receiver element picks up the echo (a very weak signal, often millivolts).
    \item A pre-amplifier (either on a commercial breakout board or built with an op-amp) amplifies the signal.
    \item The amplified signal is AC-coupled to Arduino pin A0 (ADC).
    \item The ADC samples at a high rate (e.g., 8--16 kHz) to capture the full echo transient.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Part 4: Arduino Edge Compute Implementation}

\textbf{Objective:} Implement a basic cross-correlation directly on the Arduino to estimate the time delay without relying on MATLAB.

\textbf{Note:} The Arduino has limited memory and CPU. You will implement a simplified sliding dot-product correlation that processes fewer samples and lower precision than MATLAB.

\subsubsection*{Skeleton Algorithm}

The provided skeleton \texttt{EchoCapture.ino} will be extended with a correlation block:

\begin{verbatim}
// After capturing echo samples into array 'received[]':
int max_correlation = 0;
int peak_lag = 0;

// Compute correlation for lags 0 to MAX_LAG
for (int lag = 0; lag < MAX_LAG; lag++) {
    int correlation = 0;

    // Dot product: sum(transmit[n] * received[n+lag])
    for (int n = 0; n < CHIRP_SAMPLES; n++) {
        if (n + lag < N_SAMPLES) {
            correlation += transmit[n] * received[n + lag];
        }
    }

    // Track the maximum
    if (correlation > max_correlation) {
        max_correlation = correlation;
        peak_lag = lag;
    }
}

// Convert peak lag to time delay
float delta_t_arduino = (float)peak_lag / SAMPLE_RATE_HZ;
float distance_arduino_cm = 343.0 * delta_t_arduino / 2.0 * 100.0;
\end{verbatim}

\section*{Appendix A: Cross-Correlation Mathematics (Reference)}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Appendix A: Cross-Correlation Mathematics (Reference)}
%--------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection*{A.1 Definition and Interpretation}

The discrete-time cross-correlation between signals $x[n]$ and $y[n]$ is:
\begin{equation}
R_{xy}[k] = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n] \cdot y[n+k],
\end{equation}
where $k$ is the lag (time shift in samples).

\textbf{Interpretation:} $R_{xy}[k]$ measures the similarity between $x[n]$ and a delayed version of $y[n]$. Large positive values indicate strong similarity; zero or negative values indicate poor alignment.

\subsection*{A.2 Time-of-Flight Application}

If $x[n]$ is the transmitted chirp and $y[n]$ is the received echo (with time delay $\tau$ in seconds), then the cross-correlation peaks at:
\begin{equation}
k_{\text{peak}} = \tau \cdot f_s,
\end{equation}
where $f_s$ is the sampling rate. Once $k_{\text{peak}}$ is found, the time delay is:
\begin{equation}
\tau = \frac{k_{\text{peak}}}{f_s},
\end{equation}
and the distance is:
\begin{equation}
d = \frac{v \cdot \tau}{2}.
\end{equation}

\subsection*{A.3 Why Cross-Correlation Works for Noisy Signals}

Cross-correlation is a \textbf{matched filter}. If the received signal is $y[n] = A \cdot x[n - k_0] + w[n]$ (where $A$ is attenuation and $w[n]$ is noise), then:
\begin{equation}
R_{xy}[k] = A \sum_{n} x[n] x[n + k - k_0] + \sum_{n} x[n] w[n + k].
\end{equation}

The first term peaks when $k = k_0$ (the noise term averages to near zero for uncorrelated noise). This is why cross-correlation is robust to noise.

\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Appendix B: Arduino PWM Configuration and ADC Sampling}

\subsection*{B.1 Generating a 40 kHz Signal on D9/D10}

The Arduino Uno has two PWM channels on pins D9 and D10 (Timer1, 16-bit). To generate 40 kHz:

\begin{verbatim}
// Set Timer1 to Fast PWM mode, prescaler 1, TOP = 400
// This gives: f_PWM = 16 MHz / 1 / 400 = 40 kHz
TCCR1A = 0b10100011;  // Fast PWM, output on D9
TCCR1B = 0b00001001;  // Prescaler = 1
ICR1 = 400;           // Period = 400 counts
OCR1A = 200;          // Duty cycle = 50%
\end{verbatim}

The provided skeleton handles this; you only set the frequency constant.

\subsection*{B.2 High-Rate ADC Sampling}

The Arduino's standard \texttt{analogRead()} is slow ($\sim 100 \mu$s per sample). To achieve 16 kHz sampling, you need to configure the ADC manually:

\begin{verbatim}
// Set ADC prescaler to 32 (2 MHz clock)
// This gives ~130 kHz ADC sampling, fast enough for 16 kHz acquisition
ADCSRA = 0b11100101;  // Enable ADC, prescaler = 32
ADMUX = 0b01000000;   // Select A0, reference = AREF
\end{verbatim}

The skeleton code handles this. You only adjust \texttt{SAMPLE\_RATE\_HZ} in the configuration.

\subsection*{B.3 Memory Constraints}

The Arduino Uno has 2 KB of SRAM. Two large arrays quickly consume this:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Transmit chirp: 400 samples = 800 bytes.
    \item Received echo: 2000 samples = 4000 bytes.
    \item Total: 4800 bytes, exceeds SRAM!
\end{itemize}

\noindent
\textbf{Solution:} Store the transmit chirp in program memory (PROGMEM) and limit the echo capture to 1000 samples.

\subsection*{C.2 Cross-Correlation Computation and Peak Detection}

\begin{verbatim}
% Compute cross-correlation
x_normalized = x / max(abs(x));  % normalize to [-1, 1]
y_normalized = y / max(abs(y));

[R_xy, lags] = xcorr(x_normalized, y_normalized);

% Find the peak
[peak_val, peak_idx] = max(R_xy);
peak_lag = lags(peak_idx);

% Convert to time delay
Ts = 1 / Fs;
delta_t = peak_lag * Ts;

% Convert to distance
v_sound = 343;  % m/s
distance_m = v_sound * delta_t / 2;
distance_cm = distance_m * 100;

fprintf('Peak lag: %d samples\n', peak_lag);
fprintf('Time delay: %.3f ms\n', delta_t * 1000);
fprintf('Distance: %.1f cm\n', distance_cm);
\end{verbatim}

\section*{Appendix D: Troubleshooting Guide}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Appendix D: Troubleshooting Guide}
%--------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection*{Hardware and Measurement Issues}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Transmitter produces no sound (no vibration felt).}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Verify D9 is outputting a 40 kHz signal using an oscilloscope or frequency counter.
        \item Check the coupling capacitor polarity and continuity.
        \item Verify the transmitter element is not damaged (use a multimeter to check resistance, typically 1--10 k$\Omega$).
        \item Try a different D9/D10 pin; some boards have hardware conflicts.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Receiver ADC output is always 512 (mid-rail) or flat.}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Check that the pre-amplifier is powered (check 5V at the op-amp power pins).
        \item Verify the receiver element is connected to the pre-amplifier input.
        \item Manually input a small signal (e.g., from a function generator) to the pre-amp to test gain.
        \item If gain is very high, you may be saturating the ADC; reduce pre-amp gain if adjustable.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Echo is visible on oscilloscope but not in Arduino ADC data.}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item The ADC coupling capacitor may be blocking the signal. Check DC bias of the pre-amp output (should be ~2.5 V, mid-rail).
        \item Verify the ADC sampling is actually running (check serial output for increasing sample indices).
        \item The echo may be arriving outside your capture window. Increase \texttt{N\_SAMPLES} or reduce \texttt{CHIRP\_DURATION\_US}.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Measured distances are off by a constant offset (e.g., always 5 cm too high).}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item This may be due to a systematic delay in your hardware (e.g., pre-amp propagation delay, coupling capacitor settling).
        \item Perform a calibration: measure a known distance and compute a correction factor.
        \item In MATLAB: \texttt{distance\_corrected = distance\_measured - offset}.
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Arduino Acquisition Issues}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Serial Monitor shows no output after sending trigger.}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Verify the baud rate is 115200 in both Arduino IDE and MATLAB.
        \item Check that the USB cable is properly connected.
        \item Try uploading the sketch again (sometimes the serial port loses connection).
        \item Make sure \texttt{Serial.begin(115200)} is in the setup.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{ADC data is very noisy (scattered values).}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item This is normal for breadboards. Add a 10 µF smoothing capacitor across the ADC pin and GND.
        \item Reduce the sampling rate slightly to allow the ADC more settling time.
        \item In MATLAB, apply a median filter: \texttt{y\_smooth = medfilt1(y, 5)} before correlation.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Arduino cross-correlation result is very different from MATLAB.}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Check that both are using the same transmit and receive signals (not different captures).
        \item Verify the correlation window size: Arduino may be truncating the echo.
        \item Ensure Arduino data types are not overflowing: use \texttt{long} or \texttt{float} instead of \texttt{int}.
        \item Print intermediate values (e.g., correlation at each lag) to debug.
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{MATLAB Analysis Issues}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{xcorr() produces a very small peak or multiple peaks.}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Normalize both signals before correlation to improve SNR.
        \item Check that the received signal is indeed a delayed replica of the transmit signal. If shapes don't match, the system may have distortion.
        \item Use \texttt{[R\_xy, lags] = xcorr(x, y, 'coeff')} to get correlation coefficient (normalized by signal energy).
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Peak lag is 0 or negative (indicates echo arrived before transmission).}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item This is a sign of data corruption or incorrect alignment. Verify the transmit and receive signals are from the same experiment.
        \item Check that you are not accidentally flipping the signal order (should be \texttt{xcorr(transmit, received)}, not \texttt{xcorr(received, transmit)}).
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Distance measurement jumps wildly between successive measurements.}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item The SNR may be too low. Check: does the cross-correlation peak stand out clearly, or is it buried in noise?
        \item Add pre-filtering: \texttt{y\_filtered = bandpass(y, [39000 41000], Fs)} to isolate the 40 kHz component.
        \item Ensure the object is not moving between measurements.
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{When to Ask for TA Help}

\begin{itemize}
    \item If no echo is visible on an oscilloscope (suggests transmitter failure).
    \item If Arduino data is completely flat or shows ADC clipping (saturation).
    \item If measured distances are consistently off by $> 20\%$, even after calibration attempts.
    \item If you suspect hardware damage (components are hot, board smells burned).
\end{itemize}
